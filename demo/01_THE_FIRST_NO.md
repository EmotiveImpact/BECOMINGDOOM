# Demo lesson: The First No

W01-L02 sample • editorial prototype • approximately 8 minutes including pauses and rehearsal. Validate duration by a read-through. Related daily page: W01-D03. Source context: R01, R02 and R08. All examples and exercise wording below are original.

## Outcome

The learner identifies a chosen priority, distinguishes an obligation from approval pressure and makes or rehearses one considered response.

## 0:00–1:00 | Opening

Think about the last time you said yes while hoping the other person would somehow withdraw the request.

Perhaps you were already busy. Perhaps you had finally found an hour for something important. You agreed anyway, then spent the next hour irritated with them for asking.

Return to the moment before you agreed. What did you want to protect? What did you believe would happen if you protected it?

Today we begin with a demanding proposition: your own life deserves your investment. We call that selfishness as a virtue. You are allowed to choose where your effort goes, and you are responsible for the choices you make.

## 1:00–2:00 | Principle

There are things you have freely committed to do. There are responsibilities you need to address. There are also demands you accept because you fear what saying no might make somebody think of you.

Learn to tell those apart.

If you have made a commitment, honour it or address the need to renegotiate it. If you have not, pause before accepting. Someone else's request opens a decision. It does not settle it.

The courage to be disliked begins when you allow the possibility that a considered decision may disappoint somebody.

## 2:00–3:30 | Reality: one ordinary scene

You have set aside Thursday evening to finish a portfolio. A friend asks you to spend the evening helping with a non-urgent task. You want to keep the time. You hear yourself preparing to say yes.

One response is to agree and resent them. Another is to attack them for asking. Neither gives you the clear choice you need.

Try: “I can’t help on Thursday. I’ve already set that evening aside.”

If you genuinely want to offer another time, you can. An alternative is a choice, too. You do not need to invent an excuse or describe your whole week to make the answer real.

Suppose they say: “You can move that, surely?”

You can answer: “I’m keeping that time. Thursday won’t work for me.”

Notice what the response accomplishes. It states your availability. It does not require your friend to agree that your project matters. It does not guarantee they will be pleased.

## 3:30–4:30 | Build: choose your situation

Write down a request you are considering. Use an ordinary situation with manageable consequences.

Now answer three questions. Have I already made a commitment? What would agreeing displace? If I were not trying to manage their opinion of me, what would I choose?

There may be a good reason to agree. Make that reason explicit. The purpose is to recover a considered choice.

[Pause for sixty seconds.]

## 4:30–6:00 | Practice

Write a response in one or two sentences. State the answer or limit. Give a brief explanation if useful. Offer an alternative only if you actually want to offer it.

Read it once. Remove any invented excuse. Remove any accusation that the person was wrong to ask. Check whether you have addressed an existing commitment.

Now imagine a reasonable follow-up question. Write the response you would give while keeping your chosen boundary.

[Pause for rehearsal.]

Guilt may appear. Record it as an experience, then examine whether there is an actual responsibility you have overlooked. A feeling is worth noticing; it does not make the decision for you.

## 6:00–7:00 | Field mission

Use your response in one appropriate situation. If no suitable request exists today, rehearse with a partner or draft a response to a recent situation. Do not manufacture conflict to complete an exercise.

Record what you predicted, what happened and what you did afterwards. Keep the details private where needed.

Your evidence is an honest account of a considered decision and response. Another person's approval is outside the assessment.

## 7:00–8:00 | Close and next retrieval

One clear no can protect the time for a skill, a piece of work or a life you keep postponing. What you protect must then receive your effort.

Tomorrow, explain without looking at your notes: what is the difference between an obligation and approval pressure? Then use the time you protected for the priority you named.

The boundary creates room. Your action gives that room a purpose.

## Facilitator notes

Accept a deliberate yes where it follows sound reasoning. Offer private rehearsal rather than public disclosure. Assess clarity, ownership and consequences. Do not reward hostility or pressure participants to confront someone where doing so would create a serious risk. This is educational practice, not treatment.
