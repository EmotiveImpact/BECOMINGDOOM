## The last lesson

This is the end of the course. There is no next lesson waiting, no weekly instruction arriving on Monday. Whatever you keep doing from here, you will do because you chose to.

That can feel like a loss. It is also the moment the course was built for. Six weeks of tools are only worth something if they keep working when nobody is prompting you to use them.

This final chapter is about the fact that underlies every other chapter: your time is limited, and that is what makes your choices matter.

## Four thousand weeks

Oliver Burkeman, a British writer who spent years writing a column on productivity and self-improvement, took a surprising turn in *Four Thousand Weeks* (2021). The title comes from a simple calculation: if you live to around eighty, you get roughly four thousand weeks. Put that way, a life suddenly looks short.

Burkeman argues that most time management advice makes the problem worse. It promises that if you become efficient enough, you will finally get through everything and have time left over. He calls this a trap. The more efficient you become, the more demands you attract, and the list never ends. Getting on top of everything is impossible, because there will always be more worth doing than any one life can hold.

His alternative is to accept the limit openly. You will not do everything, so choose what you will do, and accept that some good things will stay undone. He suggests that this acceptance brings relief. Once you stop trying to do it all, you can give your attention fully to the few things you have chosen. He encourages limiting how many projects you have in progress at once, deciding in advance what you will fail at, and treating the present as worth living rather than as preparation for some later, more organised life.

## Life is long enough

Burkeman was not the first to make this case. Nearly two thousand years earlier, the Roman philosopher and statesman Seneca wrote a short essay usually translated as *On the Shortness of Life*.

Seneca's argument runs against the title. Life, he says, is long enough, if we use it well. The problem is that we waste it: on busywork, on other people's demands, on chasing status, on postponing living until some future date. People guard their money and property carefully, he observes, but hand over their time to anyone who asks, as if it cost nothing.

Seneca's remedy is to treat time as the most valuable thing you own, to spend it deliberately on what you judge worthwhile, and to stop waiting for a better moment to begin. Readers have found it bracing for centuries, and it fits this course closely. Week two's lesson, every yes spends your life, is a modern restatement of his complaint.

## Embrace your finite nature

Principle fourteen says: embrace your finite nature.

It can sound gloomy. In this course it is the reason any of this matters. If you had unlimited time, no choice would cost anything, because you could do everything eventually. Because you cannot, every choice is a statement of what you value. A finite life is exactly what gives your choices weight.

This principle never means panic, and it never means inventing urgency to frighten yourself into action. It means using your limited time on purpose.

## Naming what stays undone

The hardest part of planning the next ninety days is the part Burkeman insists on: choosing one path means naming the attractive alternatives you will leave undone.

A plan that includes everything is a wish list. A real plan says what you are not doing and makes peace with it deliberately. Opportunity cost from week two and probabilistic thinking from week six do most of the work here. What does each direction push out? Which has the strongest evidence so far? Choose one sustainable milestone, then write down what waits until the next review.

## Your operating code

Over six weeks you have built a doctrine, boundaries, a command sheet, a reset, a Doombot, decision rules, a feedback loop, margins and ten thinking tools. Your operating code gathers them onto a single page so you can keep using them alone.

It has four sections. **My rules**: the doctrine and boundaries you will keep, including the no you will say most often. **My tools**: which of the ten tools you reach for, and in which situations, chosen by relevance. **My rhythm**: when you work, when you review and when you rest in a normal week. **My reset**: what you do the day after you slip, because you will slip, and the code decides in advance what happens next.

Then give it three dated reviews. At thirty days, check that the system is still running. At sixty, check the evidence against your milestone. At ninety, decide whether to continue, change or withdraw, and choose the next chapter the same way you chose this one.

## Back to the beginning

Principle one closes the loop: put yourself above all people.

Six weeks ago it may have sounded harsh. By now its meaning is clear. Your flourishing gets a real vote in how your life is spent. You protect what your future needs, deal with your responsibilities in the open and carry the consequences yourself. Your ambition gives you a claim on your own life, and on nothing beyond it. That turns out to be enough.

And become dangerous, the theme of this final week, means capable, deliberate, hard to knock off course and able to choose your response when circumstances change. It has never meant dangerous to other people.

## Two examples

A freelance designer, the same man who six weeks ago could not find one Thursday evening for his portfolio, now has it live. He has used it in three pitches and won a client at a better rate than ever before. He has stopped doing unpaid revisions. Now he has three exciting directions: grow the studio with a second designer, write a short book on branding for small businesses, or teach a weekend course for new freelancers. He runs the tools. He has a paying client and two warm leads for the studio, and no evidence yet for the book or the course. He chooses one milestone for ninety days: five clients at his new rate, and a trial week with a second designer. Then he writes the most important line on the page. The book and the course stay undone until the ninety-day review.

A woman finished the course six months ago. Her project was teaching Vietnamese cooking classes in her flat, and by the end of the six weeks she had run three paid classes. By month four her sessions had drifted to one a week. There was no crisis, only a busy spell at her day job, a family wedding and a cold that lasted a fortnight. Her sixty-day review was in the calendar, so it caught the drift. Her reset told her what to do: notice, record, repair, resume. She wrote one line about what had happened, cut a committee she had joined because she could not think how to refuse, restored two sessions a week and booked the next review. The code did not stop the drift. Nothing would have. It brought her back much faster than she would have managed alone.

## Where people go wrong

**No next step.** The course ends and so does the practice. Six weeks of momentum vanish in three.

**Everything at once.** Three big goals started together, none finished.

**No reviews.** Nobody checks in at thirty, sixty or ninety days, including you. Put the dates in the calendar now.

**The next course.** Finishing one programme and immediately looking for another, because being taught feels like progress. What you need now is ninety days of running your own code.

## Further reading

- **Oliver Burkeman, *Four Thousand Weeks* (2021).** A warm, funny and clear-eyed book about time management for finite people. Perhaps the best single book to read after this course.
- **Seneca, *On the Shortness of Life*.** A short essay, about two thousand years old, that still reads as if written for the age of the smartphone. Many modern translations are available.
