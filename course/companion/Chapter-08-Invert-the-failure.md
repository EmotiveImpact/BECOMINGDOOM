## Planning upside down

Most planning asks how to succeed. This chapter turns the question over and asks how your project is going to fail.

It sounds pessimistic. In practice it is one of the fastest ways to make something work, and it is how people in dangerous professions think every day.

Imagine it is Doomsday, the end of week six. You stand up to show your project and it does not work. Something has gone badly wrong. What killed it? Answer quickly. Your first few answers are usually the most useful, because they are the ones you already half know and have been avoiding.

## "Invert, always invert"

The fourth thinking tool is inversion, and its most quoted advocate is a nineteenth-century mathematician. Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi, a German who made major contributions to several branches of mathematics, is said to have advised his students to "invert, always invert". Many hard problems, he found, became easier when approached backwards: assume the opposite of what you want to prove, or work from the answer towards the question.

The idea reached a wide modern audience through Charlie Munger, the long-time business partner of Warren Buffett, who made inversion a centrepiece of his thinking. Munger liked to point out that it is often easier to avoid stupidity than to achieve brilliance. Instead of asking how to be successful, he would ask what reliably causes failure, and then avoid those things.

Shane Parrish's *The Great Mental Models, Volume 1* (2019) presents inversion as a core tool for exactly this reason. Working out what you want to avoid is often clearer and more actionable than working out what you want to achieve.

In this course the question is: **what would make this fail, and what can I prevent?**

The second half matters as much as the first. Inversion is never just a list of fears. Every risk ends with an action.

## The pre-mortem

The most useful form of inversion has a name. Gary Klein, an American psychologist known for his research on how experts make decisions under pressure, described the pre-mortem in a short article in the *Harvard Business Review* in 2007.

A post-mortem asks why something died after it is already dead. A pre-mortem asks the same question while you can still do something about it. The team imagines that the project has failed completely, then each person writes down every reason they can think of for the failure. Klein argued that framing the failure as something that has already happened frees people to be honest, because they are explaining an event rather than criticising a plan, and it surfaces risks that ordinary optimistic planning tends to bury.

This course uses the pre-mortem in three steps. **Set the scene**: it is Doomsday and the project has failed, and you write as if it already happened. **List the causes**: fast and specific, technical, personal and practical, in about ten minutes. **Rank and act**: choose the three most likely causes and give each one a preventive action with a date.

## The checklist

Atul Gawande, an American surgeon and writer, made the case for a humbler kind of inversion in *The Checklist Manifesto* (2009). Gawande argued that in complex work, failure usually comes from two sources: not knowing enough, and failing to apply what we already know. The second is far more common than people admit. Skilled professionals forget steps, skip checks and assume someone else has done something.

His remedy, drawn from aviation and then tested in operating theatres, is the checklist: a short list of the critical steps that are easy to miss. Pilots run checklists before every flight, however experienced they are. Gawande worked with the World Health Organization on a surgical safety checklist, and studies in a range of hospitals found that it was associated with fewer complications and deaths.

A checklist is inversion made routine. Someone once asked how things go wrong, and wrote the answers down so that nobody has to remember them under pressure.

## How projects die

When people run a pre-mortem on a personal project, three causes come up again and again.

**Nobody can open it.** A broken link, the wrong permissions, a file that only works on your laptop, a demo that needs wifi in a room without any.

**Nobody asked for it.** It was built for an audience that turned out not to exist, or that wanted something slightly different.

**Nobody finished it.** The scope grew faster than the hours, until week six arrived with most of four things and none of one.

Each of these is preventable, and each is far cheaper to prevent than to fix on the day.

## The limit of the tool

Inversion has a trap. Risk listing can become avoidance. Some people love it because it feels responsible: they spend an evening imagining disasters and never build anything. That is fear dressed up as planning.

Keep it short. Ten minutes, three risks, three actions, then back to work.

## Choosing what to practise

Inversion has a second use this week. Ask which of your weaknesses is most likely to hurt the project, and choose that as your subskill to practise. Choose the skill most likely to cost you, even if another would be more fun to work on.

That is principle seven, vow to become a master, in its most practical form: deliberate practice on one small thing, with feedback.

## Two examples

An IT contractor is building a simple dashboard for his clients and wants to add automatic alerts this week. He runs inversion first and the answer is immediate: the client cannot open it, because he has only tested it on his own laptop, logged in as himself. Instead of adding the feature, he spends an hour testing on a phone, a tablet and a relative's laptop as a test client. He finds that the page breaks on phones and that a client account can see a menu it should not. Either would have ended the demonstration, and the second would have been a real security problem. He fixes both, then adds the alerts the following week.

A council housing officer is giving his first conference talk. His pre-mortem lists three failures: the slides will not load, he runs over time, he blanks on the opening. His preventions: slides on a memory stick and in his email; two timed rehearsals; the first two sentences memorised. On the day the venue laptop will not read his file. The stick works on the second machine. He finishes a minute early, and nobody in the room knows how close it came. Good prevention usually looks like nothing happening.

## Where people go wrong

**The doom loop.** Imagining failure until you no longer want to start.

**Vague risks.** "It might not work" cannot be prevented. Name the mechanism.

**No dates.** A preventive action without a date is a worry written neatly.

**Preventing the wrong thing.** Working hard to prevent the most frightening failure instead of the most likely one.
