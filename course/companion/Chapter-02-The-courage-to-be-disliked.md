## The fastest word in the language

Most people can say yes faster than they can think. A request arrives, and before any check against the calendar, the bank balance or the plan written yesterday, the answer is already out. Yes, of course. No problem. Happy to.

That speed is the subject of this chapter. A yes that arrives before a decision was never really a decision. It was a reflex, trained over years, usually with good intentions. Most of us learned early that agreeable children are praised, that helpful colleagues are valued, and that the person who says no is the one people talk about afterwards. The training worked. For many adults, refusing even a small request feels physically uncomfortable, as if something important is at risk.

Chapter 1 asked you to write a doctrine: what you are building, what it needs and what it will cost. This chapter is about the moment that doctrine first meets another person who wants the time you protected.

## A Japanese bestseller about an Austrian psychiatrist

The title of the lesson comes from *The Courage to Be Disliked*, by the Japanese philosopher Ichiro Kishimi and the writer Fumitake Koga. First published in Japan in 2013, it became a bestseller across Asia before reaching English readers several years later.

The book is written as a dialogue over five nights between a philosopher and an unhappy young man who has come to argue with him. Through their conversations it introduces the ideas of Alfred Adler, the Austrian psychiatrist who worked alongside Freud before breaking with him to found what he called individual psychology.

Several of Adler's ideas, as the book presents them, sit close to the heart of this course.

The first is that much of our unhappiness is bound up with our relationships with other people: comparison, competition and the constant wish to be approved of. The second is that the desire for recognition, however natural, can quietly take over a life. If you need other people's approval to feel your choices are valid, you end up living by their expectations, and the life you build is theirs.

The third idea, and the most practical, is what the book calls the separation of tasks.

## Whose task is it?

The separation of tasks asks a simple question about any situation: whose task is this? Or, put another way, who will ultimately live with the consequences of this choice?

Your decision about how to spend Wednesday evening is your task. How a friend feels about that decision is their task. You can be kind about it, you can explain it, you can offer an alternative. You cannot make their feelings your responsibility without also making yourself responsible for everything they might ever feel, which is impossible and exhausting.

This distinction is liberating once it lands. Another person's disappointment tells you something true about their plans and their hopes. It tells you nothing about whether your decision was right. When the two get tangled, people end up managing everyone's emotions and owning nobody's results, least of all their own.

Kishimi and Koga's provocative conclusion is that freedom involves accepting that some people will dislike you. Living so that nobody is ever disappointed means never acting on your own judgement. The courage in the title is the willingness to let someone be displeased with a decision that was yours to make.

## Burning the bridge of permission

Principle eight of our creed says: burn the bridge of permission. It is one of the more dramatic lines in the doctrine, and it is easy to misread.

It does not mean burning relationships, and it does not mean ignoring permissions that are genuinely required. Your employer's policies, the terms of a contract, consent from people your actions affect: those are real, and a serious person respects them.

The bridge we burn is a different kind of permission. The imaginary sign-off. The feeling that someone, somewhere, must agree that your ambition is reasonable before you are allowed to protect it. Nobody is going to issue that permission. There is no office that approves priorities. If you wait for everyone to agree that your project deserves the evening, the evening will go to whoever asks first.

Imaginary permission tends to hide in three places. **Approval**: waiting for someone to agree that your priority is reasonable. **Explanation**: believing a no only counts if it comes with a reason the other person accepts, which turns every refusal into a trial you have to win. And **comfort**: saying yes so that nobody has to feel awkward for a few seconds, including you.

## The joy of saying no

Natalie Lue, a British writer who has written about people-pleasing for many years, comes at the same problem from a practical angle in *The Joy of Saying No* (2022). Her starting point is that people-pleasing is a habit, usually learned for good reasons in childhood, rather than a fixed trait. Habits can be relearned.

Lue's work treats boundaries as a way of reconnecting what you do with what you value. A boundary, in this view, is information about you: what you can offer, what you cannot, and where your limits sit. People who never state their boundaries gain little in generosity and become harder to know, because nobody can tell which of their yeses are real.

That point matters for anyone who worries that saying no will damage their relationships. The relationships most worth keeping are the ones that can survive an honest answer.

## Three ways a refusal goes

When a request lands in protected time, the reply tends to take one of three shapes.

**The biography.** You explain everything: the deadline, how tired you are, the history of the project, how bad you feel. It seems polite. In practice it hands the other person a list of arguments. Surely the project can wait one night? Every reason you give is a door they can try to push open, and each long apology teaches them that your time is open to negotiation.

**The accusation.** You make them wrong for asking. You protect the hour and damage a relationship that did nothing to deserve it. People who have spent years as pushovers sometimes swing here when they first find their nerve.

**The clean no.** You state your availability once, without blame and without a lecture, and offer a genuine alternative if there is one. "I can't tonight, I'm working on something I've committed to. I could do Saturday morning." It is the shortest of the three, and usually the kindest, because the help it offers will actually arrive.

## What happens next

Here is something worth knowing before you try this. Most people are poor at predicting how others will react to a boundary. We imagine anger, hurt or a lasting chill in the friendship. Far more often the response is a shrug, a thumbs-up or a simple "no worries".

Do not take that on trust. The exercise for this lesson is to write down the reaction you expect before you say no, and the reaction you actually get afterwards. Over a few weeks that record becomes evidence, and evidence is far more persuasive than reassurance.

## Two examples

A freelance designer has protected Wednesday evening for his portfolio. At ten to seven a good friend asks for help packing for a house move. It is a reasonable favour from someone he likes, which is exactly what makes it hard. He can pack all evening and let the doctrine collapse on its first test. He can write four paragraphs of apology and leave both of them feeling guilty. Or he can say he cannot do tonight and offer three hours on Saturday morning. His friend replies with a thumbs-up. On Saturday he turns up and works hard, and nobody mentions Wednesday again.

A team leader loses an hour four mornings a week to a call where nothing gets decided. She tells the group she will join on Mondays and read the notes on the other days. One sentence, no apology. A colleague grumbles. Her manager asks whether everything is all right, and she says she is using the time for the quarterly plan. She gets three mornings back each week. The disapproval she feared lasts about a day.

## Where people go wrong

**The essay.** Every extra reason weakens the refusal. One short reason is stronger than three.

**The fake maybe.** "Let me check," when you already know the answer. It only delays the no and makes it heavier when it arrives.

**The cold no.** Clarity without warmth. A curt reply to a friend who asked politely is a different failure, but it is still a failure.

**The over-correction.** Discovering the clean no and then using it on everything, including commitments you genuinely owe. Principle one says your interests count. So do the promises you have already made. A boundary protects a priority. It gives no licence to walk away from your responsibilities.

## A note of caution

Practise on low-stakes requests first: a meeting that could be an email, a favour that can move to another day. Never test a new boundary on a genuine emergency or on someone who is vulnerable and depending on you. The aim is to build a skill in proportion, the same way you would build strength, one sensible step at a time.

## Further reading

- **Ichiro Kishimi and Fumitake Koga, *The Courage to Be Disliked*.** An accessible introduction to Adlerian psychology, written as a dialogue. The separation of tasks is the idea most worth taking away.
- **Natalie Lue, *The Joy of Saying No* (2022).** A practical, warm guide to unlearning people-pleasing and setting boundaries that reflect your values.
