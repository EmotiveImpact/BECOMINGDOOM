# Chapter 17: Preserve room to recover

*The Becoming Doom reading companion • Week 6: Become Dangerous • Goes with video lesson W06-L02*

*Drawing on Benjamin Graham and Nassim Nicholas Taleb. The ideas are credited to them; the arguments and examples in this chapter are ours.*

## Something will go wrong

Doomsday is close. You have a project, evidence and a demonstration planned. On the day, something will probably not go to plan. A file will not open, a guest will cancel, the internet will drop, a cold will arrive the night before.

You cannot stop all of that from happening. You can decide in advance how much it is allowed to hurt.

## The bridge and the river

Engineers design bridges to carry far more than the heaviest load they expect. They do this because their estimates might be wrong, materials vary and traffic changes over the decades. The cost of being slightly wrong is too high to leave to chance.

That spare capacity is a margin of safety. The tenth and final thinking tool in this course asks you to build it into your plans, and its question is: **what buffer or fallback keeps a plausible setback manageable?**

Notice the two key words. *Plausible*: you prepare for what is likely, plus anything that would be fatal, and leave the rest. *Manageable*: the aim is to make a setback survivable. Making it impossible is beyond anyone.

## An investor's rule

The phrase itself comes most famously from Benjamin Graham, the British-born American investor and teacher often described as the father of value investing. In *The Intelligent Investor*, first published in 1949 and revised several times, he devoted his final chapter to it and called it the central idea of sound investment.

Graham's argument was that nobody can forecast the future precisely, so a sensible investor should only buy something when its price sits well below a careful estimate of its real value. That gap is the margin of safety. If your estimate turns out to be too optimistic, or bad luck strikes, the gap absorbs the error and you are still all right. Without it, you need to be right about everything, and nobody is.

Graham was writing about shares and bonds, but the logic travels well. Any plan rests on estimates: how long something will take, how much it will cost, how many people will turn up. A margin is room for those estimates to be wrong.

## The value of spare capacity

Nassim Nicholas Taleb, whose *Antifragile* appeared in Chapter 13, makes a related argument about redundancy. Modern life, he suggests, is obsessed with efficiency: stripping out every spare minute, every spare pound and every backup, because spare capacity looks like waste. On a normal day it is. On a bad day it is the only thing that matters.

Taleb points to the human body, which carries two kidneys and two lungs when one of each would do on an ordinary day. He contrasts people and businesses with no slack, who can be wiped out by one shock, with those who keep reserves and can survive the shock, and sometimes take advantage of it while others struggle.

The connection to Graham is direct. Both argue that the future will surprise you, and that the sensible response is to keep room for the surprise.

## Three kinds of margin

In practice a margin usually takes one of three forms.

**Time.** A spare day before a deadline. Arriving an hour early. Finishing the main feature a week before the demonstration.

**Resources.** Money set aside for emergencies. A second copy of a file. A friend who can lend equipment.

**A smaller version.** A plan B that still delivers the core of what matters if plan A fails. A five-minute demo instead of fifteen. A pilot instead of a launch.

The third is often the most useful, because it costs the least. You do not need to buy a second of everything. You need to know which part of your work is the core, and be able to deliver that part on its own.

## The limit of the tool

Buffers have costs and cannot remove every risk. Every spare day is a day you did not use for something else. Every pound in reserve is a pound you did not invest. And no amount of preparation covers everything, so trying to is its own failure. Too much margin eats the very work it was meant to protect. Graham never suggested refusing to invest. He suggested investing with room for error. The same balance applies here.

## Two principles

Principle eleven, master the art of the tactical withdrawal, returns from week four. A fallback is a planned retreat. When the full version fails, you withdraw to the smaller one deliberately and without drama, and keep going on ground you can hold.

Principle fourteen, embrace your finite nature, sets the boundary. Your time is finite, so you cannot prepare for everything. A margin of safety is the discipline of choosing which few setbacks deserve your preparation, and accepting the rest. Hope is not a plan, and neither is endless preparation.

## Two examples

A man in his early thirties has built a small web tool for community gardens: volunteers sign up for watering shifts and coordinators can see which plots need help. Three gardens have used it for a fortnight. The night before his demonstration he runs a margin check. He has a copy that runs on his laptop without the internet. He writes a five-minute version that shows only the sign-up and the coordinator's view. He saves twelve screenshots of real use. Ten minutes before he presents, the hosting service the tool depends on goes down. He tells the room, opens the local copy, shows the core in five minutes, then the screenshots. He is calm because the decision was made the night before. In an earlier week he had made the opposite mistake, building five backup plans in a panic and never finishing the main feature. Proportion is the skill.

A self-employed plumber's van broke down last March. It took a week to fix. He lost a week of jobs, paid the garage on a credit card, and two regular clients found someone else. This year he built a margin: one month's business costs kept in a separate account he does not touch for anything else, and an arrangement with another tradesman to lend each other a spare van at short notice. In October the van breaks down again, with the same garage and the same kind of fault. He borrows the van the next morning, pays the garage from the buffer and loses one day. He keeps every client. The setback was identical. The damage was a fraction of what it had been, because he prepared while things were calm.

## Where people go wrong

**Zero slack.** A plan that only works if nothing goes wrong. It looks efficient and it is fragile.

**Endless preparation.** So many backups that the real work never gets done. Preparation can become a way of avoiding the frightening part.

**The spent buffer.** Using the margin on ordinary days, so it has gone when the bad day arrives. The emergency fund pays for a takeaway. The spare day before Doomsday fills up with a new feature.

**The secret buffer.** A fallback kept in your head and never tested. A backup you have never opened is a hope. Open the local copy. Run the small demo once.

## Further reading

- **Benjamin Graham, *The Intelligent Investor* (1949, revised 1973).** A classic of investing. Read the final chapter on margin of safety even if you never invest a penny.
- **Nassim Nicholas Taleb, *Antifragile* (2012).** Revisit the sections on redundancy and the costs of over-optimisation.
