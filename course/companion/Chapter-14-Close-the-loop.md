## "I love it"

You show someone your project and they say they love it. It feels wonderful. You go home lighter and tell someone it went really well.

Then comes the harder question. Did they use it? Did they finish the task it was built for, come back the next day, pay, book or tell a friend without being asked?

Praise describes how people feel in front of you. Behaviour describes what they do when you are not there. Only behaviour tells you whether your project works.

## Everything that improves has a loop

A cyclist watches his power output and adjusts his effort. A chef tastes the sauce and adds salt. A thermostat reads the temperature and switches the heating on. Signal, check, adjust, over and over. Anything that gets better over time has a loop like this, whether or not anyone designed it.

The eighth thinking tool in this course is feedback loops, and its question is: **which signal leads to which adjustment, and when?**

It has three parts. A **signal** you can observe. A **review interval**, so you actually look at it. And an **adjustment rule**, so looking changes something. Most people have only the first part. They collect information and never decide what it means.

## Build, measure, learn

Eric Ries, an American entrepreneur, set out the most influential modern version of this idea in *The Lean Startup* (2011). Drawing on his experience of start-ups that failed after years of building products nobody wanted, he proposed a different approach: treat a new venture as a series of experiments.

At its heart is a loop he called build, measure, learn. Build the smallest version of the product that lets you test a key assumption, which he called a minimum viable product. Measure how real people respond. Learn whether the assumption was right, and decide whether to persevere or pivot to a new approach. The goal is what Ries called validated learning: evidence, gathered quickly and cheaply, about what customers actually value.

Ries also drew a sharp distinction that this chapter depends on. Vanity metrics, such as total sign-ups, page views or downloads, tend to go up and make founders feel good, but they rarely guide decisions. Actionable metrics show clear cause and effect: did this change make more people complete the task, return or pay? Optimise the first kind and you can feel successful while your product quietly fails.

## Ask about their life

Rob Fitzpatrick, a British founder, wrote *The Mom Test* (2013) about a specific problem: how to learn from conversations with potential customers when everyone, especially people who like you, wants to be kind.

The book's title comes from its central idea. Your mother loves you and will tell you your business idea is wonderful. Ask her the wrong questions and you learn nothing. Ask the right ones and even she cannot mislead you.

Fitzpatrick's rules for the right questions are simple. Talk about their life rather than your idea. Ask about specific things that have happened in the past rather than opinions about what they might do in the future. Listen more than you talk. "Would you use an app that did this?" invites a polite yes. "When did this last cause you a problem, and what did you do about it?" invites the truth. He is especially wary of compliments, which feel like progress and contain almost no information.

## A useful signal

Taken together, these books point to three qualities in a good signal.

**Behaviour.** It records what people do, which is much harder to fake than what they say, including to themselves.

**Tied to the result.** It moves when the real outcome moves. If your project exists to help people keep a habit, the signal is whether they keep it, not how often they open the app.

**Cheap to read.** You can check it in minutes each week. A signal that takes a day to measure will stop being measured.

## The limit of the tool

Avoid optimising a vanity measure instead of the intended result. Likes, compliments and downloads feel like progress, and they are very easy to chase. The loop only works if the signal is honest about the thing you actually care about.

## Accountability and chaos

Two principles return here. Principle nine, radical accountability, means you own the result your users actually get, not just the praise they give. Praise is pleasant and you can keep it. The job is the result.

And principle ten, manipulate chaos into currency, finds its most practical form. Every drop-off, complaint and confusing result is a small piece of chaos. A good loop turns it into currency: specific information about exactly what to fix next. Bad news is often the most useful news you will get.

## Two examples

A call-centre worker who taught himself to code builds a small habit-tracking app and gives it to ten testers. The messages are glowing. Then he asks two testers if he can watch them set it up, without helping. Both get stuck on the same screen, which seems to demand an account before you can choose a reminder. When he checks, only three of the ten have finished setting up. He picks one signal (set-up completion), one review day (Friday) and one rule (under half means fix the screen where people stall). He redesigns that single screen. The next Friday, seven of ten complete it, and he moves on to the next signal.

A woman who runs a neighbourhood book club hears that it is the best part of everyone's month. The signal says otherwise: attendance falls from twelve to nine to six. Her rule, set when she started, is that under eight she asks the people who stopped coming. Most say the same thing: the books are too long, and they felt embarrassed turning up halfway through. She switches to shorter books, with the occasional long one given two months. Attendance returns to eleven. The kind words never told her that. The empty chairs did.

## Where people go wrong

**No review date.** Data collected and never looked at.

**Changing everything at once.** Five changes in a week, so you cannot tell which one worked.

**Asking only friends.** Kind people are poor measuring instruments.

**Moving the threshold.** Deciding the rule was too strict because you did not like the result. Change a rule deliberately at a review, never in reaction to an answer.
