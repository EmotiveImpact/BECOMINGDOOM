## The question every empire must answer

Look at what you have built so far. A doctrine and boundaries. A system that survives disruption. A capability tested and improved. The ability to hold your ground with other people.

Week five asks the question every empire eventually faces: does your work produce real value for someone else? It starts with leverage.

## Same hours, same you

Your hours, money and attention are limited, and that will not change much in the next two weeks, or probably the next two years. So the question is where one of those scarce resources could produce far more useful output than it does now. Where could one hour do the work of ten?

A lever is the oldest machine there is. With a long enough bar and a fixed point to push against, one person can move a weight they could never lift with their arms. Archimedes is said to have claimed that, given a place to stand, he could move the world. Work behaves the same way. Some effort moves things a little. Some effort, placed in the right spot, moves a great deal and keeps moving after you stop pushing.

## Leverage in the modern economy

Naval Ravikant, an American entrepreneur and investor, became widely known for a series of posts and podcasts about building wealth and living well. Eric Jorgenson collected and edited them into *The Almanack of Naval Ravikant* (2020).

Ravikant describes several forms of leverage. Labour: other people working for you. Capital: money working for you. And what he calls the newer forms, code and media, which he regards as the most powerful because they need nobody's permission. Software and content can be copied endlessly at almost no extra cost, so a piece of work made once can serve thousands of people while you sleep.

He pairs leverage with two other ideas: specific knowledge, the kind of skill that is hard to train and comes from your own curiosity and experience, and accountability, putting your name to your work. In his account, real advantage comes from combining all three.

You do not need to share his goals to use the idea. The most useful question it raises is this: which parts of your work could be made once and then keep producing value?

## The tool and its question

The seventh thinking tool in this course is leverage, and its question is: **where can a scarce resource produce more useful output?**

The key word is *useful*. Leverage is measured by the result the person at the other end receives. How busy you look, how impressive your tools are and how many hours you put in do not count.

Leverage tends to come in four forms. **Clarity**: write something once, clearly, so you never have to explain it again. **Systems**: a template, checklist or process others can follow without you, as Michael Gerber argued in *The E-Myth Revisited*, introduced in Chapter 9. **Tools**: software that repeats a clear task. **People**: help, paid or swapped, on what others do better.

Notice the order. Clarity usually comes first, because every other lever multiplies whatever it is given. A system built on confusion spreads confusion, and a tool fed unclear instructions repeats them to more people, faster.

## Know the problem first

Before multiplying anything, make sure you know the problem your audience actually has: the one they describe, in their own words, when you ask. This is first principles from Chapter 7 returning. Leverage applied to an assumed problem just produces the wrong thing faster.

## The limit of the tool

Automating bad work scales bad work. And every lever carries hidden costs that must be counted before you build it. Quality: will it be as good as doing it by hand? Maintenance: who updates it when things change? A chatbot nobody maintains is wrong within months. A checklist with no owner goes stale.

## Chaos into currency

Principle ten of the creed says: manipulate chaos into currency.

The idea has a close cousin in the work of Nassim Nicholas Taleb, a Lebanese-American essayist and former options trader. In *Antifragile* (2012), Taleb argued that the opposite of fragile is something more than robust. A fragile thing breaks under stress. A robust thing survives it. An antifragile thing gets stronger: muscles under training, some businesses in a crisis, systems that learn from every small failure. Taleb argued that people and organisations should arrange themselves to benefit from volatility, uncertainty and disorder rather than merely enduring them.

This course takes the principle in a modest, practical form. Find a useful response inside the mess. Repeated questions, constant interruptions, contradictory instructions: most people put up with them or complain. Someone using this principle turns them into an asset that saves time for everyone who comes after.

It never means exploiting other people's distress, and it does not assume that every problem can be turned into money. Sometimes the currency is time, trust or calm.

## Build your empire

Principle five says: build your empire. In this course an empire is built from useful assets, systems and fair exchanges. A clear guide is an asset. A good checklist is an asset. Each keeps producing value after the effort that made it is over.

It does not mean your worth is measured in money, and nobody is promised a fortune. Some people will build a business. Others will build a better role at work, a community project or a body of creative work. Any of those is an empire if it is built from useful things.

## Two examples

An operations manager at a charity that has grown from twelve staff to thirty spends about five hours with every new starter answering the same fifteen questions. Her first idea is a chatbot. But the existing instructions are scattered across four folders, two of which contradict each other, so a bot would give confident, confusing answers to more people. Instead she sits with recent starters, writes down their actual questions, and produces a clear two-page checklist with links to the right documents. The next starter asks three questions instead of fifteen. She fixes those three. Only then will she consider automating the stable parts.

A personal trainer with twenty-two clients gets the same food questions by text, often late at night. He collects a month of messages and finds that most are versions of the same eight. He writes a two-page guide answering them and films three short videos. Within a month, by his own count, the late-night texts have roughly halved. His clients get better answers at any hour, and he gets his evenings back.

## Write it as a hypothesis

Before building any lever, write it in four lines. Today it costs this much. I will change this one thing. I expect this result. I will know by this date. Leave the result as a question mark until reality fills it in. Chapter 14 builds the loop that checks it.

## Where people go wrong

**Busy tools.** Software that looks impressive and saves nobody time.

**Scaling the mess.** Multiplying a process before it works.

**Forgotten upkeep.** Ignoring the cost of maintaining the lever.

**The wrong audience.** A lever that saves you time but makes your audience's experience worse.
