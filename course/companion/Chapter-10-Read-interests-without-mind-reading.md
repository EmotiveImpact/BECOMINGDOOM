## Your project meets other people

For three weeks most of your work has happened on your own. You chose, protected, built and tested. Week four is where your project meets the people who can say yes or no to it: clients, colleagues, managers, partners and suppliers.

It begins with a skill almost everyone believes they have and very few practise: reading what the other person actually wants.

## Arguing with a ghost

Before a difficult conversation, most of us rehearse a story about the other person. In the car, in the shower, at three in the morning. They only care about price. They want to catch me out. They have already decided.

Ask yourself how you know. Usually the honest answer is that you guessed. You took a few facts, filled the gaps with your own fears, and then walked into the room arguing with someone who exists only in your head.

## Interests, not positions

The most influential book ever written about negotiation began as a research project at Harvard. *Getting to Yes* (1981), by Roger Fisher and William Ury of the Harvard Negotiation Project, set out what the authors called principled negotiation, and its ideas have shaped how negotiation is taught ever since.

Its most important distinction is between positions and interests. A position is what someone says they want: a lower price, a later deadline, a particular office. An interest is why they want it: the budget they must stay within, the boss they must satisfy, the fear of being blamed if something goes wrong. Positions often clash directly. Interests frequently do not, and once they are on the table, options appear that neither side could see while arguing about positions.

The book offers a famous illustration. Two people argue over whether a library window should be open or shut. One wants fresh air; the other wants to avoid a draught. Arguing about the window gets nowhere. Opening a window in the next room solves both problems.

Fisher and Ury add three other principles: separate the people from the problem, invent options for mutual gain, and insist on objective criteria. They also introduced the idea of a BATNA, your best alternative to a negotiated agreement, which tells you when walking away is better than any deal on offer. That idea returns in Chapter 12.

## Incentives

The fifth thinking tool in this course builds on the same insight. The question is: **what outcomes might matter to each party, and how can I check?**

Look for three things. **Reward**: what gets this person praised, paid or promoted? **Fear**: what would get them blamed if it went wrong? Fear often explains more than reward. The person blocking your proposal may simply be the one who gets the phone call when things fail. **Measure**: which number is their boss watching? If their target is speed, they will trade money for time. If it is cost, they will trade time for money.

Behaviour that seems unreasonable usually makes sense once you can see one of these three.

## Know, infer, ask

All of this goes onto an interest map with three columns.

**Know**: what the other person has actually said or done, which you could point to. **Infer**: your best guess about what they want, useful and reasonable, but still a guess. **Ask**: the question that would turn your guess into evidence.

The middle column is where the danger lives. The limit of this tool is that an inferred motive is not a fact. Write your inferences down and label them honestly. The moment you forget a guess was a guess, you start negotiating with a ghost.

## Questions as a tool

Chris Voss spent much of his career as a hostage negotiator for the FBI, and in *Never Split the Difference* (2016), written with Tahl Raz, he argued that the techniques used in kidnapping negotiations apply to everyday business and personal life.

Voss is critical of purely rational models of negotiation, arguing that people are driven by emotion far more than they admit. His approach centres on what he calls tactical empathy: understanding and acknowledging the other side's feelings and perspective, not to agree with them, but so they feel heard and become more open.

Several of his tools are directly useful here. One is the calibrated question: an open question, usually beginning with "how" or "what", that invites the other person to explain their situation and think about your problem. "What would make this work for you?" "How am I supposed to do that?" Another is labelling: naming the emotion you sense, as in "it sounds like timing is the real worry", and then listening.

Voss and Fisher and Ury disagree on some things, but they agree on the most important one. You learn what the other side wants by asking well and listening properly, rather than by assuming.

## Killer instinct

Principle three of the creed says: develop your killer instinct. In this course it means acting decisively on a real opportunity and being willing to hear no.

The opening has to be seen before it can be taken. That is why this lesson sits under the principle. Killer instinct is the decisive move that follows good reading. It has nothing to do with aggression, intimidation or charging in. A good boxer watches before he strikes.

## Two examples

An independent logistics consultant goes into a renewal meeting with his biggest client convinced they will demand a discount. He has prepared fifteen per cent off to offer before they ask. His interest map says something different. He knows they renewed last year without negotiating and have complained twice about slow reports. He infers price. He asks, before any numbers: what would make this year better than last year for you? The new operations director answers immediately: speed, because they are measured on how quickly they respond to problems. He proposes faster turnaround at the same price and a premium tier for urgent work. They take both. He leaves with more income, because he asked one question before he mentioned money.

An events coordinator wants a raise in a company that has had a poor year. She expects a flat no and has prepared a long argument. Instead she asks her manager what would make her role more valuable this year. The manager's real worry is cover for a team lead about to go on leave. They agree that she takes on those responsibilities, with a raise attached and a review when the lead returns, all in writing. The question found an interest the argument would have missed.

## Where people go wrong

**Projection.** Assuming the other person wants what you would want.

**Interrogation.** Firing question after question until the meeting feels like a cross-examination. Two or three good questions are plenty.

**Not listening.** Asking, then waiting for your turn to speak.

**Manipulation.** Using what you learn against people. Find the interest so you can offer something that serves both sides.
