## The session that starts an hour late

You sat down on time. You opened the laptop. Then you checked one message, then one more thing, then made a drink, then remembered something you needed to look up. The session began at eight. The work began at nine.

Most people blame themselves for this. They call it a lack of discipline and promise to try harder. Then they sit in the same room, with the same phone beside them and the same notifications on, and get the same result.

This chapter argues that the problem is usually the room. Your surroundings make thousands of small decisions before you get the chance to. A phone within reach gets checked. A vague task gets postponed. A cluttered desk invites other things. Discipline is far easier when the room is on your side, and the room is something you can build.

## A Stoic sorting tool

The second thinking tool in this course is control and influence, and its oldest ancestor is about two thousand years old.

Epictetus was born into slavery in the first century AD, in what is now Turkey. After gaining his freedom he taught Stoic philosophy, first in Rome and later in Greece. He wrote nothing himself, but his student Arrian recorded his teachings, and a short handbook of them, the *Enchiridion*, has been read ever since.

The handbook opens with the idea that made Epictetus famous: some things are up to us and some things are not. Our judgements, choices and responses are up to us. Our bodies, our reputations, other people's actions and the events of the world are, in the end, not. Much human misery, he taught, comes from confusing the two: trying to control what we cannot, and neglecting what we can.

This course uses a practical, three-part version of that idea. The question is: **what can I decide, what can I influence, and what can I only respond to?**

You decide where your phone is, when you start, where you sit and what the first task is. You can only influence whether the people you live with keep the noise down, or whether your manager moves a meeting: you can ask, but you cannot guarantee it. And some things you can only respond to: a late train, a sick child, a broken boiler.

The limit on this tool is that influence is partial. Do not pretend you control everything, and do not collapse into believing you control nothing. Both mistakes are comfortable, and both are costly.

## If this, then that

The most useful practical tool in this chapter comes from research rather than philosophy.

The German psychologist Peter Gollwitzer, working with colleagues including Paschal Sheeran, spent decades studying what he called implementation intentions: plans that link a specific situation to a specific response, in the form "if this happens, then I will do that". A general goal says "I will work on my project this evening". An implementation intention says "if the flat gets loud, then I will put on headphones and do the shorter version of the session".

Across many studies, plans written in this form have been found to help people act on their intentions more reliably than goals alone. The effects depend on the context and the person, and they are no guarantee. But the mechanism makes intuitive sense. The plan moves the decision out of the difficult moment, when you are tired and distracted, and into a calm one, when you can think clearly. When the interruption arrives, you do not need to deliberate. You have already decided.

## Designing the room

James Clear's *Atomic Habits* (2018) has become one of the bestselling books on behaviour change, and much of its practical value lies in its attention to environment. Clear argues that people overrate motivation and underrate the design of their surroundings. If you want to do something more often, make it obvious and easy: put the guitar in the middle of the room, leave the running shoes by the door. If you want to do something less, make it invisible and difficult: put the phone in another room, delete the app.

Clear also draws on the research into implementation intentions, and his suggestion to attach new habits to existing ones, "after I make coffee, I will write one paragraph", is a close relative of the if-then plan.

From these ideas this course takes four conditions almost anyone can decide. **Place**: the same spot, ready before you need it. **Time**: a fixed start in the calendar, never "when I feel ready". **First task**: written the night before and small enough to begin at once. **Distraction**: phone out of reach, notifications off, old tabs closed. None of these needs willpower in the moment. You set them once, while calm, and the session runs on the set-up.

## Despise comfort, wisely

Principle thirteen says: despise comfort and seek the friction. It sounds like a call to suffer, and two recent books show why it can be read more carefully.

Michael Easter's *The Comfort Crisis* (2021) argues that modern life has removed so much discomfort (hunger, cold, boredom, physical effort, uncertainty) that many people have lost the benefits those experiences once brought. Easter, an American journalist, built the book around a gruelling hunting trip in the Alaskan wilderness and makes the case for deliberately reintroducing proportionate challenge.

Ryan Holiday's *The Obstacle Is the Way* (2014) draws on the Stoics, particularly the Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius, to argue that difficulties can be turned into the means of progress. The obstacle in the path, approached well, becomes the path.

In this chapter the principle has a very practical meaning. Put friction in front of distraction, and remove friction from in front of the work. A phone in another room takes effort to check, and that effort is working for you. A card with your first task already written removes the effort of starting.

Despising comfort means refusing to let the easy default make your decisions. It does not mean despising rest, and it never means making yourself suffer for its own sake.

## Two examples

A junior software tester shares a flat and cannot guarantee a quiet evening. Relying on willpower, the first burst of laughter from the kitchen ends his session. Demanding total control, he sends tense messages to the flat chat and spends the evening managing other people. With the if-then approach, he sets the four conditions he decides, asks his flatmates once and politely for quieter evenings twice a week, and prepares a response to the likeliest interruption: headphones and a twenty-five-minute version of the session.

A bus driver on rotating shifts has watched every fixed-evening plan collapse by Wednesday. He cannot control the rota, so he stops trying. Instead he keeps a packed bag with his notebook and tablet, and follows one rule: forty minutes on the project within two hours of waking, whatever the shift. The schedule changes every week. The rule does not.

## Where people go wrong

**Fighting the weather.** Pouring energy into what you can only respond to.

**The perfect set-up.** Buying a new desk, a new app and a new notebook, and still not starting. The set-up becomes the work.

**No plan B.** Writing off a whole evening after one interruption. A shorter session still counts.

**Controlling people.** Using "influence" as permission to boss others. Influence means asking. The people you live with keep the final say over their own evenings.

## Further reading

- **Epictetus, *Enchiridion*.** Short enough to read in an hour and useful for a lifetime. Several good free translations exist.
- **James Clear, *Atomic Habits* (2018).** The most practical modern guide to habits and environment design.
- **Michael Easter, *The Comfort Crisis* (2021).** A lively case for proportionate, chosen discomfort.
- **Ryan Holiday, *The Obstacle Is the Way* (2014).** Stoicism applied to setbacks.
- **Peter Gollwitzer's research on implementation intentions.** The research overviews are a good starting point for the evidence behind if-then plans.
