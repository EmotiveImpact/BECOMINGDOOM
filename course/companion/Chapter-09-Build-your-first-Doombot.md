## Machines that carry your will

In the Marvel comics, Doctor Doom builds Doombots: robotic doubles that carry out his orders, take the risks he would rather avoid and handle the repetitive work while he concentrates on what only he can do. It is one of the character's most recognisable traits, and the one this chapter borrows.

Your first Doombot will be much less dramatic and much more useful. It is any aid that takes repetition off your hands while you keep the judgement.

## Where repetition hides

Look back over the last two weeks and list what you did more than three times. Writing nearly the same email. Formatting the same report. Copying figures from one place to another. Running through the same checks before every session. Answering the same question from different people.

Every hour spent on work that is the same each time is an hour not spent on the work only you can do: the thinking, the craft, the relationships and the judgement.

## A definition

In this course, a Doombot is **a bounded, repeatable aid that you can check**.

*Bounded*: it does one defined job, and you know exactly where that job starts and stops. *Repeatable*: it works the same way next week and the week after. *Checkable*: you can tell quickly and reliably whether its output is right.

It can be a template, a checklist, a spreadsheet, a saved prompt for an AI tool or a small automation. The technology matters much less than the boundary and the check.

## Working on the business

Michael Gerber's *The E-Myth Revisited* (1995) is one of the most influential books ever written for small business owners, and its central argument fits this chapter closely.

Gerber argued that most small businesses are started by technicians: people who are good at a particular kind of work, such as baking, plumbing or accounting, and who assume that being good at the work is the same as being able to run a business that does it. The result, in his account, is an owner trapped doing everything personally, working long hours in the business and never on it.

His alternative is to build the business as if it were going to be franchised: to turn the work into documented systems that produce consistent results whoever runs them. The owner's job shifts from doing every task to designing, testing and improving the systems that do them.

You do not need to plan a franchise to take the lesson. Every time you turn a repeated task into a checkable system, you move a little of your time from working in your project to working on it. That is the first glimpse of principle five, build your empire, which arrives fully in week five. Empires run on systems that keep working when you are not watching every step.

## Checklists as machines

Chapter 8 introduced Atul Gawande's *The Checklist Manifesto* (2009) as a form of inversion. It belongs here too, because a checklist is the simplest Doombot there is.

Gawande distinguished between two kinds of checklist. A "do-confirm" checklist lets experienced people work from memory, then pause to confirm that nothing was missed. A "read-do" checklist is followed step by step. He also stressed that good checklists are short, precise and focused on the steps that are both critical and easy to skip. A checklist that tries to capture everything becomes so long that nobody uses it.

That is a useful rule for any Doombot. Make it do one job well, and keep it small enough that you will actually use it.

## A good first job

A good first Doombot job has four qualities. It is **frequent**, done at least weekly, so the saving builds up. It is **structured**: the shape is the same each time and only the details change. It is **checkable**: you can tell in a minute or two whether the output is right. And it is **low stakes**: a mistake costs minutes, not a client. You earn the right to automate bigger things by running smaller ones well.

Bring the other tools from this week to it. First principles asks what the task actually requires. Inversion asks how the Doombot could fail, and what you can prevent.

## The rule about private data

One rule matters more than any time saving. Private information about clients, patients, customers or anyone else does not go into a tool unless you know where it is stored, who can see it, and that you are allowed to put it there. When in doubt, anonymise: names become "Client A", and personal details come out entirely.

People trust you with their information. A Doombot that leaks it betrays that trust on your behalf.

## Two examples

A freelance marketing consultant writes a weekly progress report for each of five clients, about two hours each. She builds a helper: a fixed template in her house style, plus a saved prompt that turns her rough notes into a first draft. She then has three choices. Trust it completely and send whatever it writes, which is fast until the week it invents a figure. Never build it, which is safe and slowly expensive. Or draft, then verify: the helper produces a draft in ten minutes and she checks every figure against her source data and edits the wording, finishing in forty minutes instead of two hours.

The owner of a small cleaning firm spends ninety minutes every Sunday night matching six cleaners to twenty jobs by hand. His Doombot is a spreadsheet with a few simple rules: postcode area, available hours, and who cannot work where. It drafts the rota in seconds. He reviews it in ten minutes and fixes two clashes the rules missed. The spreadsheet carries the repetition. He keeps the judgement, because only he knows that one cleaner's mother is unwell this week.

## Test it properly

Never decide that a Doombot works because it feels clever. Run it on one task where you already know the right answer. Do the same task by hand. Compare the two against a short rubric written in advance: accuracy, time taken and how much editing it needed. Then decide to keep it, change it or discard it. Discarding is a perfectly good result. You learned something cheaply.

## Where people go wrong

**Too broad.** "Do my admin" is not a job. "Draft the weekly invoice from these notes" is.

**No check.** Sending output nobody has verified.

**Private data.** Putting personal information into tools you do not control.

**Automating the mess.** A confused process, automated, produces confusion faster. Fix it on paper first.
