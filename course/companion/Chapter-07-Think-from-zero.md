## From fortress to laboratory

Two weeks in, you have a doctrine, a first no, a priced calendar, a room built for work and a reset for bad days. That is the fortress. Week three changes the question from "can I keep going?" to "what can I actually do?"

The laboratory is where capability gets built: a real thing, tested and improved with feedback. It begins by stripping your project back to what it truly requires.

## The weight of things you never needed

Projects rarely fail because their owners are lazy. They stall under the weight of things nobody needed. The custom website. The perfect logo. The course that must cover everything before anyone sees it. The five-year forecast for a business with no customers.

Most of those requirements are borrowed. We look at someone further along, notice everything they have, and assume we need it all before we can start. What we are seeing is the result of years. We cannot see which parts do the real work and which were added later simply because they could be.

## Reasoning from the ground up

The third thinking tool is first principles. The phrase goes back to Aristotle, who described first principles as the basic propositions from which other knowledge is derived and which cannot themselves be deduced from anything else. In modern use, thinking from first principles means breaking a problem down to what is fundamentally true and building up from there, instead of reasoning by analogy from what others have done.

Shane Parrish, founder of the Farnam Street website, includes first principles thinking in *The Great Mental Models, Volume 1* (2019) as one of the core tools for clear thinking. His account stresses that the method is about separating what we know to be true from the assumptions and conventions layered on top of it, so that we can see options others miss.

This course turns that into a single question: **what is required, what is observed, and what is merely assumed?**

*Required* means the project fails without it. *Observed* means you have seen real evidence for it: someone told you, showed you or paid you. *Assumed* means you believe it and have not checked. Most of any plan lives in that third category.

## Three passes

First principles runs in three passes.

**The job.** Write the single job the project must do, for one specific person, in one sentence.

**The sort.** Mark every item in your plan as required, observed or assumed. Be strict: if you cannot point to the evidence, it is assumed.

**The cut.** Park everything assumed on a list called "later". Parked, not deleted. It returns the moment a test shows it matters.

What remains is usually much smaller than expected and much closer to finished.

## The limit of the tool

First principles is easy to misuse. Some people treat it as permission to ignore experts and reinvent everything, which is a slow way of rediscovering what everyone already knew.

Do not throw away accumulated expertise without a reason. Some conventions exist because they work. Accountants follow certain formats because regulators require them. Photographers show complete weddings because couples want to see a whole day. First principles asks you to know why something is there before you copy it, and to be honest when you do not know.

## Mastery is built in contact

The principle for this week is P07: vow to become a master. Two books shape how this course understands it.

Robert Greene's *Mastery* (2012) studies the lives of figures such as Leonardo da Vinci, Charles Darwin and Mozart, alongside contemporary practitioners, to describe a path to mastery. Greene emphasises a long apprenticeship, learning from mentors, immersing yourself in the practice of a field and absorbing its rules deeply enough to eventually break them creatively. The path he describes is measured in years, often a decade or more.

Anders Ericsson, the Swedish psychologist whose research on expert performance spanned four decades, wrote *Peak* (2016) with the science writer Robert Pool. Ericsson's work is often linked to the "ten thousand hours" rule, which he spent years correcting. Hours alone, he argued, do not create expertise. What matters is deliberate practice: focused effort on specific weaknesses, at the edge of your current ability, with clear goals and immediate feedback, usually guided by someone who knows what good looks like.

Both books point the same way. Mastery is built in contact with reality: with teachers, with feedback and with the people who use the work. Polishing in private feels like progress because you are busy and things are getting prettier, but it teaches you little.

And both are honest about time. Six weeks will not make anyone a master of anything. Six weeks can make you someone who has started properly, with real feedback and a record of improvement. That is the vow.

## Opportunity cost returns

Bring back the first tool from week two. Every feature you add has a price, and very often that price is contact with real people. Three weekends building a website are three weekends without a single conversation with a potential client. Months spent planning the perfect studio are months without teaching, which is the only thing that would make anyone a better teacher.

## Two examples

A wedding photographer wants better-paid clients and has spent three weekends building a custom website with animated menus and a booking system. It is still not live. Thinking from first principles, he asks what an engaged couple actually needs in order to decide whether to contact him: to see weddings like theirs done well, a rough idea of price and a way to get in touch. Everything else is assumed. He puts up a simple page with three full weddings, a starting price and a contact button by Friday, then shows it to two engaged couples and asks which wedding would make them call. Both choose the same one, and it is not the one he thought was his best. That one answer reshapes his portfolio.

A trained yoga teacher has not started because her plan includes a studio lease, a brand, an app and professional photography. Stripped back, a class needs a room, a time, some people and a way to pay. She books a community hall for one Saturday. Nine people come and four book the next class before they leave. Now she has evidence, and the studio can wait.

## Where people go wrong

**Cutting quality.** Removing what makes the work good instead of what makes it big. Make it smaller, never worse.

**Fake observed.** Promoting one friend's opinion to evidence.

**Never rebuilding.** Refusing to add something a test shows is genuinely needed, out of attachment to minimalism.

**The clever rebel.** Using first principles as an excuse to ignore every expert.
