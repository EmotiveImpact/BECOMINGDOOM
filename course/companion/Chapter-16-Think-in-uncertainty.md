## One good result

Something has gone well. A client loved your work, a product sold out, a post took off. The temptation is to read it as proof and act on it at once: resign, sign the lease, go all in.

The opposite temptation is just as common. You call it luck, decide it will never happen again and carry on as before.

Both reactions feel decisive. Both treat a single result as if it settled the question. This chapter is about a third way: holding a belief with the amount of confidence the evidence actually supports, and saying in advance what would change it.

## The world deals in odds

Very few things in a working life are certain. A pitch might land. A client might stay. A shop might pay its rent in February. Most people talk about these outcomes as if they were yes or no, will or won't. Reality deals in odds.

The ninth thinking tool in this course is probabilistic thinking, and its question is: **what outcomes are plausible, and what evidence would update my confidence?**

That question has two halves. The first asks you to picture a range of outcomes, more than one. The second asks you to name, before the evidence arrives, what would move you. Most of the value lies in the second half.

## Ordinary people who forecast well

Philip Tetlock, a psychologist who has spent decades studying expert judgement, wrote *Superforecasting* (2015) with the journalist Dan Gardner. The book grew out of a large forecasting tournament in which thousands of volunteers made predictions about world events: elections, conflicts, economic figures. Their forecasts were scored against what actually happened.

A small group did consistently well. Tetlock and Gardner called them superforecasters, and the book's most encouraging finding is that they were mostly not famous experts. They were ordinary people with particular habits of mind.

The habits are what matter for this course. Good forecasters broke big questions into smaller ones they could estimate. They started from how often similar things had happened before, then adjusted for the details of the case. They updated their views often, usually in small steps, as new information arrived. They were comfortable saying they were unsure, and they treated their beliefs as hypotheses to test rather than positions to defend.

None of that requires a mathematical gift. It requires honesty and a willingness to change your mind.

## Decisions and outcomes

Annie Duke, whose book *Quit* appeared in Chapter 12, made her first argument about decisions in *Thinking in Bets* (2018). Drawing on her years as a professional poker player, she argues that people constantly confuse the quality of a decision with the quality of its outcome.

She calls this mistake resulting. A good decision can lead to a bad result because of luck, and a bad decision can lead to a good one. A poker player who makes the right call and loses the hand has still played well. A player who makes a reckless call and wins has still played badly, and will lose over time if they keep doing it.

Duke suggests a simple test for any belief: would you bet on it? Imagining money on the table tends to make people more honest about how sure they really are. A claim that felt certain often turns out, under that pressure, to be a feeling of about sixty per cent.

For this chapter the lesson is plain. One good outcome may tell you less about your decision than it feels like it does.

## The base rate

Daniel Kahneman, the psychologist whose work on judgement won him a Nobel Prize in economics, described in *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011) how people tend to ignore base rates: the general frequency of something in the wider world. Faced with a vivid individual case, we judge it on its own story and forget to ask how often cases like it usually turn out well.

Kahneman described two ways of looking at a plan. The inside view focuses on the details of your own situation and tends to be optimistic. The outside view asks what happened to other people who tried something similar, and tends to be more accurate. Good judgement usually starts with the outside view and then adjusts.

In practice the base rate question is easy to ask and easy to forget. Before you read too much into your own result, find out what usually happens to people in your position.

## The confidence statement

This lesson asks you to write a confidence statement about your Doom Project. It has three parts.

**Your confidence**, in plain words: low, medium or high, or a range such as low to medium. **Your evidence**: what that confidence is based on, so someone else could check it. **Your update condition**: the specific evidence that would move your confidence up or down, and by when.

The update condition is the part that does the work. It turns a vague hope into a test with a date on it.

## The limit of the tool

Numbers without a basis are false precision. Saying "seventy-three per cent" sounds rigorous, but unless you can say where the number came from, it is a guess dressed as a fact, and it can mislead you and everyone you show it to. Tetlock's forecasters used numbers because they were scored over hundreds of predictions. For most of us, most of the time, plain words are more honest. Low, medium and high, with the evidence written beside them, are enough.

## Two principles

Principle fourteen says: embrace your finite nature. Your time is limited, which is exactly why you cannot afford to bet all of it on one data point, and why you cannot afford to ignore a real signal either. Calibration is how a finite person spends time well. This principle never means inventing urgency to push yourself into a leap. It means using limited time carefully.

Principle four returns as well: hold a vision and be ruthless. The vision stays fixed. The ruthlessness is aimed at your own wishful thinking. You can want something very badly and still be honest about how likely it is.

## Two examples

A maths teacher with twenty years in a secondary school wants to build a private tutoring practice. His first student, the daughter of a friend of a friend, goes up two grades in her mocks. Her mother tells everyone, and two enquiries follow. He is tempted to resign next month. Instead he writes a confidence statement: low to medium that tutoring could replace his salary, based on one strong result, two enquiries and a known need locally. His update condition is five regular paying clients by the end of March, at least three of them from people he did not already know. Until then he keeps teaching, takes the two enquiries and builds the evidence. His ambition is unchanged. He has simply stopped betting his savings on a single result.

A woman sells her own West African sauces at a weekend market. Two summer Saturdays in a row she sells out, and a café asks to stock them. She wants to sign a twelve-month lease on a shop. First she checks the base rate, asking three traders she trusts how their takings change through the year. All three say the same: summer is busy for everyone, and January and February are the real test. Her confidence that a shop would pay drops to medium, perhaps low. Her update rule is to keep the stall running through the winter. If takings stay above half the summer level and the café order continues, she signs in March. If not, she tries a smaller step, such as a shared kitchen or a second market.

## Where people go wrong

**Fake precision.** A confident percentage with nothing behind it. Ask where any number came from.

**Story over data.** One vivid success outweighing many quiet failures. The client who loved it is a story. The nine who never replied are data.

**Resulting.** Judging a decision only by how it turned out, so that luck teaches you the wrong lesson.

**Never updating.** Treating your first estimate as a promise you must defend. Changing your mind when the evidence changes is the point of the whole exercise.
