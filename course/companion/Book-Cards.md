# Becoming Doom: book cards

*The Becoming Doom reading companion • One short card for every book on the course reading list*

*Each card says, in our own words, what the book is about, the ideas the course takes from it, where it appears and what to watch for. The cards reproduce none of the books' text. The ideas belong to their authors; read the originals for the full arguments.*

---

## How to use these cards

The cards follow the course in order, grouped by the week in which each book first appears. Each one names the lessons and companion chapters it supports, so you can move between the video, the chapter and the book. Where a book returns later in the course, the card says so.

A few cards carry a caution. These are books the course draws on with care: a philosophical argument rather than a research finding, a popular idea whose evidence is debated, or sales advice that can be misused. The caution says how we use the book and where we stop.

## Week 1: Claim the Throne

### Ayn Rand, *The Virtue of Selfishness* (1964)

**What it is.** A collection of essays by the Russian-American novelist and philosopher, setting out her ethics of rational self-interest.

**The ideas we take.** A person's own life and flourishing can be a legitimate moral purpose. Self-interest, properly understood, means long-term rational interest and rules out sacrificing other people. Living for others' approval is a form of self-betrayal.

**Where it appears.** W01-L01 and Chapter 1, which give principle one, put yourself above all people, its meaning.

**Caution.** This is a philosophical argument and a contested one. We use it for the idea that your flourishing deserves a vote, and leave the wider politics alone. Read it critically.

### Greg McKeown, *Essentialism: The Disciplined Pursuit of Less* (2014)

**What it is.** A practical book by a British-born writer and former consultant on doing fewer things better.

**The ideas we take.** Every yes is a trade-off, whether or not you admit it. Clear priorities make refusals easier. Saying no gracefully is a skill you can learn.

**Where it appears.** W01-L01 and Chapter 1 (principle four, hold a vision and be ruthless), then W02-L01 and Chapter 4 (opportunity cost).

### Ichiro Kishimi and Fumitake Koga, *The Courage to Be Disliked* (2013; English 2017)

**What it is.** A Japanese philosopher and a writer present the psychology of Alfred Adler as a conversation between a philosopher and a sceptical young man.

**The ideas we take.** The separation of tasks: work out whose task something is, and stop taking on other people's. Living for approval means living someone else's life. Freedom has a price, and part of it is being disliked by some people.

**Where it appears.** W01-L02 and Chapter 2, which take their title from the book, then W04-L03.

### Natalie Lue, *The Joy of Saying No* (2022)

**What it is.** A British writer's guide to unlearning people-pleasing.

**The ideas we take.** People-pleasing is a learned habit rather than a personality. Boundaries express your values and can be kind. A no can be short, warm and final.

**Where it appears.** W01-L02 and Chapter 2, and the boundary scripts throughout the course.

### Albert Camus, *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942)

**What it is.** A philosophical essay by the French-Algerian writer on how to live in a world that offers no guaranteed meaning.

**The ideas we take.** The absurd: the clash between our hunger for meaning and a universe that does not supply it. The answer Camus gives is to live fully and with defiance, and to stop waiting for meaning to be handed down. His image of Sisyphus content with his boulder is one of the most famous in philosophy.

**Where it appears.** W01-L03 and Chapter 3 (principle two, no inherent meaning).

### Viktor Frankl, *Man's Search for Meaning* (1946)

**What it is.** An Austrian psychiatrist's account of surviving Nazi concentration camps, followed by an outline of the therapy he developed afterwards.

**The ideas we take.** Even in terrible conditions, a person keeps the freedom to choose their attitude. Meaning is found through work, love and the way we face suffering. A purpose gives people the strength to endure.

**Where it appears.** W01-L03 and Chapter 3, as the counterweight to Camus: meaning is made by choosing.

### Carol Dweck, *Mindset* (2006)

**What it is.** An American psychologist's popular account of her research on beliefs about ability.

**The ideas we take.** People who believe ability can grow tend to respond better to setbacks than people who see it as fixed. Effort and strategy matter, and so does the way we talk to ourselves about failure.

**Where it appears.** W01-L03 and Chapter 3 (principle six, believe in yourself).

**Caution.** Later studies have found smaller effects than the early work suggested. We use the idea as a useful habit of thought and make no claim about its size.

## Week 2: Build the Fortress

### Jocko Willink and Leif Babin, *Extreme Ownership* (2015)

**What it is.** Two former US Navy SEAL officers apply lessons from combat to leadership in business.

**The ideas we take.** Leaders own everything in their world, including the failures. Blaming others feels good and fixes nothing. Simple plans and clear priorities beat clever ones under pressure.

**Where it appears.** W02-L01 and Chapter 4 (principle nine, radical accountability), and again in Week 5.

### Epictetus, *Enchiridion* (around AD 125)

**What it is.** A short handbook of Stoic teaching, compiled by a student of the Greek philosopher who had once been enslaved.

**The ideas we take.** Some things are within our control, chiefly our judgements and actions, and many are not. Peace and effectiveness come from putting our effort where it counts.

**Where it appears.** W02-L02 and Chapter 5 (control and influence).

### James Clear, *Atomic Habits* (2018)

**What it is.** An American writer's practical guide to building habits.

**The ideas we take.** Small habits compound. Environment shapes behaviour more than willpower does. Make good habits obvious and easy, and bad ones invisible and hard. Identity follows repeated action.

**Where it appears.** W02-L02 and Chapter 5 (building conditions for execution).

### Michael Easter, *The Comfort Crisis* (2021)

**What it is.** An American journalist's argument that modern life is too comfortable for our own good.

**The ideas we take.** Chosen, proportionate discomfort, such as hard physical challenges, boredom and time outdoors, can build resilience. Constant comfort carries hidden costs.

**Where it appears.** W02-L02 and Chapter 5 (principle thirteen, despise comfort).

### Ryan Holiday, *The Obstacle Is the Way* (2014)

**What it is.** An American writer's popular application of Stoic ideas to setbacks.

**The ideas we take.** Perception, action and will: see the obstacle clearly, act on what you can, and accept what you cannot change. Obstacles can become the route forward.

**Where it appears.** W02-L02 and Chapter 5.

### Brad Stulberg and Steve Magness, *Peak Performance* (2017)

**What it is.** A writer and a running coach set out what top performers in many fields have in common.

**The ideas we take.** Growth comes from stress plus rest, in a cycle. Recovery is part of the work. Routines and purpose help people sustain effort over time.

**Where it appears.** W02-L03 and Chapter 6 (principle twelve, the body as the ultimate weapon).

### Peter Attia, *Outlive* (2023)

**What it is.** An American physician's long view of health, written with Bill Gifford.

**The ideas we take.** Train for the life you want at eighty. Strength, fitness, sleep and emotional health all matter, and small consistent habits beat dramatic efforts.

**Where it appears.** W02-L03 and Chapter 6.

**Caution.** The course gives no medical advice. Use the book for perspective and see a doctor about anything specific.

## Week 3: Enter the Laboratory

### Robert Greene, *Mastery* (2012)

**What it is.** An American author's study of how masters in many fields developed, built from biographies.

**The ideas we take.** Mastery follows a long apprenticeship. Find work that connects to your deep interests, learn from mentors and practise for years.

**Where it appears.** W03-L01 and Chapter 7 (principle seven, vow to become a master).

### Anders Ericsson and Robert Pool, *Peak* (2016)

**What it is.** The Swedish psychologist who studied expert performance for decades, writing with a science journalist.

**The ideas we take.** Deliberate practice: focused effort at the edge of your ability, with clear goals and fast feedback. Expertise is built more than it is born.

**Where it appears.** W03-L01 and Chapter 7, and the practice blocks in Week 3.

### Shane Parrish, *The Great Mental Models, Volume 1* (2019)

**What it is.** The first in a series from the Canadian founder of the Farnam Street website, introducing core thinking tools.

**The ideas we take.** First principles: break a problem down to what you know is true and build up from there. Inversion: ask what would guarantee failure, then avoid it. A small set of reliable tools beats a large set you never use.

**Where it appears.** W03-L01 and Chapter 7 (first principles), W03-L02 and Chapter 8 (inversion). The course's ten thinking tools are shaped by this tradition.

### Atul Gawande, *The Checklist Manifesto* (2009)

**What it is.** An American surgeon and writer on why simple checklists save lives in medicine, aviation and building.

**The ideas we take.** Skilled people still make predictable mistakes, especially under pressure. A short, well-designed checklist catches them. Good checklists are brief and tested in real use.

**Where it appears.** W03-L02 and Chapter 8, then W03-L03 and Chapter 9 (the Doombot).

### Michael Gerber, *The E-Myth Revisited* (1995)

**What it is.** An American small-business adviser on why so many small businesses struggle.

**The ideas we take.** Many owners are skilled technicians trapped in their own businesses. Work on the business as well as in it. Write systems that let others deliver consistent results without you.

**Where it appears.** W03-L03 and Chapter 9 (the Doombot), and again in W05-L01 and Chapter 13 (systems as leverage).

## Week 4: Command the Room

### Roger Fisher and William Ury, *Getting to Yes* (1981)

**What it is.** The classic book on principled negotiation from the Harvard Negotiation Project. Later editions add Bruce Patton.

**The ideas we take.** Separate the people from the problem. Focus on interests behind positions. Invent options for mutual gain. Know your best alternative to a negotiated agreement.

**Where it appears.** W04-L01 and Chapter 10 (incentives and interests), W04-L02 and Chapter 11 (options), W04-L03 and Chapter 12 (your walk-away point).

### Chris Voss with Tahl Raz, *Never Split the Difference* (2016)

**What it is.** A former FBI hostage negotiator on lessons from high-stakes negotiation.

**The ideas we take.** Listening and empathy are tactical skills. Calibrated "how" and "what" questions draw people out. Labelling the other side's feelings lowers tension.

**Where it appears.** W04-L01 and Chapter 10 (principle three, develop your killer instinct, applied as asking well).

**Caution.** Some of the techniques can slide into manipulation. The course uses the listening and questioning, always in good faith.

### Howard Marks, *The Most Important Thing* (2011)

**What it is.** The co-founder of Oaktree Capital Management distils decades of memos to his clients.

**The ideas we take.** Second-level thinking: go past the obvious answer and ask what follows, what others already believe and what could go wrong. Think in ranges of outcomes rather than single predictions.

**Where it appears.** W04-L02 and Chapter 11 (second-order consequences).

### Sun Tzu, *The Art of War* (probably 5th century BC)

**What it is.** An ancient Chinese text on strategy, attributed to a general and probably shaped by many hands.

**The ideas we take.** The best victory needs no fight. Know yourself and your opponent. Choose ground you can hold, and treat withdrawal as a strategic move.

**Where it appears.** W04-L03 and Chapter 12 (principle eleven, master the art of the tactical withdrawal).

### Annie Duke, *Quit* (2022)

**What it is.** A former professional poker player on the skill of knowing when to stop.

**The ideas we take.** Most people quit too late. Sunk costs and fear of looking like a quitter keep us in losing positions. Set kill criteria in advance, before pressure clouds judgement.

**Where it appears.** W04-L03 and Chapter 12 (decision rules for continue, change or withdraw).

## Week 5: Build the Empire

### Eric Jorgenson, *The Almanack of Naval Ravikant* (2020)

**What it is.** The ideas of the American entrepreneur and investor Naval Ravikant on wealth and happiness, collected and edited by Eric Jorgenson.

**The ideas we take.** Leverage comes in forms such as labour, capital, code and media. Specific knowledge, gained through genuine curiosity, is hard to compete with. Put your name to your work.

**Where it appears.** W05-L01 and Chapter 13 (leverage, principle five, build your empire).

**Caution.** The course makes no claims about income. We use the idea of leverage to ask where effort produces more useful value.

### Nassim Nicholas Taleb, *Antifragile* (2012)

**What it is.** A Lebanese-American essayist and former options trader on things that gain from disorder.

**The ideas we take.** Some things break under stress, some survive it and some get stronger. Redundancy looks like waste on good days and saves you on bad ones. Over-optimised systems are fragile.

**Where it appears.** W05-L01 and Chapter 13 (principle ten, manipulate chaos into currency), then W06-L02 and Chapter 17 (margin of safety).

### Eric Ries, *The Lean Startup* (2011)

**What it is.** An American entrepreneur's method for building new ventures through rapid experiments.

**The ideas we take.** Build, measure, learn. Test assumptions with the smallest possible version, the minimum viable product. Separate vanity metrics from actionable ones.

**Where it appears.** W05-L02 and Chapter 14 (feedback loops).

### Rob Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test* (2013)

**What it is.** A British founder's short guide to learning from customer conversations.

**The ideas we take.** Ask about people's lives and past behaviour rather than your idea. Compliments contain almost no information. Specific facts about the past beat opinions about the future.

**Where it appears.** W05-L02 and Chapter 14.

### Alex Hormozi, *$100M Offers* (2021)

**What it is.** An American entrepreneur's framework for building offers people want.

**The ideas we take.** The value of an offer rises with the size of the result and the buyer's belief they will get it, and falls with the time and effort it demands. A clear offer is often more important than marketing.

**Where it appears.** W05-L03 and Chapter 15 (making the offer).

**Caution.** The course rejects fake scarcity, false deadlines and inflated guarantees. Urgency has to be real, and nothing here promises income.

### Seth Godin, *This Is Marketing* (2018)

**What it is.** An American writer's argument that good marketing is a form of service.

**The ideas we take.** Choose the smallest viable audience and serve it well. Make something that genuinely helps a specific group of people, and tell them about it respectfully.

**Where it appears.** W05-L03 and Chapter 15.

## Week 6: Become Dangerous

### Philip Tetlock and Dan Gardner, *Superforecasting* (2015)

**What it is.** A psychologist and a journalist on what a large forecasting tournament revealed about good judgement.

**The ideas we take.** Good forecasters are mostly ordinary people with good habits. They break questions down, start from base rates, update in small steps and hold beliefs as hypotheses.

**Where it appears.** W06-L01 and Chapter 16 (probabilistic thinking).

### Annie Duke, *Thinking in Bets* (2018)

**What it is.** Duke's first book on decision-making, drawn from poker.

**The ideas we take.** Resulting: judging a decision by its outcome, when luck plays a part. Ask whether you would bet on a belief to test how sure you really are.

**Where it appears.** W06-L01 and Chapter 16. Her later book, *Quit*, appears in Week 4.

### Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011)

**What it is.** A Nobel-winning psychologist's account of the two modes of thought, fast and intuitive or slow and deliberate, and the biases they produce.

**The ideas we take.** People neglect base rates when a case has a vivid story. The outside view, asking how similar plans usually turn out, tends to be more accurate than the inside view.

**Where it appears.** W06-L01 and Chapter 16.

**Caution.** Some studies the book cites have not held up well under later replication. The base-rate and outside-view ideas remain widely used.

### Benjamin Graham, *The Intelligent Investor* (1949, revised 1973)

**What it is.** The classic guide to value investing, by the British-born American investor and teacher.

**The ideas we take.** Margin of safety: only act when there is room for your estimates to be wrong. Nobody forecasts precisely, so build the gap in.

**Where it appears.** W06-L02 and Chapter 17 (margin of safety).

**Caution.** Nothing in the course is financial advice.

### Oliver Burkeman, *Four Thousand Weeks* (2021)

**What it is.** A British writer's book on time management for finite people.

**The ideas we take.** An eighty-year life is roughly four thousand weeks. Efficiency never clears the list, so choose what matters and accept what stays undone. Limit how much you have in progress at once.

**Where it appears.** W06-L03 and Chapter 18 (principle fourteen, embrace your finite nature).

### Seneca, *On the Shortness of Life* (around AD 49)

**What it is.** A short essay by the Roman Stoic philosopher and statesman.

**The ideas we take.** Life is long enough if we use it well. People guard their money and give away their time. Stop postponing life until a better moment.

**Where it appears.** W06-L03 and Chapter 18, echoing week two's lesson, every yes spends your life.
