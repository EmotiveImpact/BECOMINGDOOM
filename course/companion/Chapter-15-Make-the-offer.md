## Where the empire meets the market

You know your audience, their problem in their own words, your lever and your signal. Now you put something in front of them and ask them to commit.

For many people this is the moment the whole course has been quietly preparing them for: asking for something clearly and being ready for any answer.

## Offers with no edges

Listen to how most people offer their work. Let me know if I can help. Happy to chat any time. Reach out if you ever need anything. Open for work.

These sound generous and nearly always produce nothing. They ask for nothing and offer nothing specific. The person reading would have to work out what you do, what it costs, how long it takes and what to do next. Most people will not do that work. They scroll on.

A real offer has edges. Think of the menu outside a café or the price list on a barber's wall: you know what you get, what it costs and what to do next. When an offer has edges, people can say yes to it, or no, which is also useful.

## The value of an offer

Alex Hormozi, an American entrepreneur who built and sold a chain of gyms before turning to investing and teaching, set out his approach to offers in *$100M Offers* (2021).

Hormozi's central argument is that most businesses struggle because their offer is weak, not because their marketing is poor. He proposes a way to think about the value of any offer from the buyer's point of view. Value rises with two things: the size of the result the buyer wants (the dream outcome) and how likely they believe they are to get it. Value falls with two others: how long they must wait for the result, and how much effort and sacrifice it demands of them. Improve any of those four and the offer becomes more attractive, often without changing the price.

The framework is useful even for very small offers. Ask whether the buyer can see the result clearly, believe they will get it, get it quickly and get it without too much effort on their part.

A word of caution. Some sales advice, including some built on these ideas, leans on invented scarcity, fake deadlines and exaggerated guarantees. This course does not. Urgency has to be real. If you say there are five slots, there are five slots.

## Marketing as service

Seth Godin, an American writer on marketing, offers a gentler complement in *This Is Marketing* (2018). Godin argues that good marketing is a form of service: making something that genuinely helps a specific group of people and telling them about it in a way that respects them.

One of his most practical ideas is the smallest viable audience. Instead of trying to appeal to everyone, choose the smallest group of people you can serve brilliantly and design your offer for them. Being specific feels risky, but it is what makes people recognise that an offer is meant for them.

Hormozi and Godin have very different styles, and they would disagree on plenty. They agree that a clear, specific offer made to the right people beats a vague one made to everyone.

## Small and clear

The rule for this week is that a small, clear offer beats a big, vague promise. You can always expand a pilot that works. You cannot easily shrink a promise you made to everyone. Start with something small enough to deliver brilliantly, clear enough to say yes to and bounded enough that both sides know when it ends.

## Five parts, one page

A complete offer has five parts and fits on one page.

**The result**, in your audience's words: what will be different afterwards. **The scope**: what is in, and just as importantly, what is out. **The time**: a start and an end. **The cost**: stated plainly, even if it is free, because free still costs time and attention. **The ask**: the exact thing you want them to do next.

If any of the five is missing, the reader has to guess, and every guess is a reason to do nothing.

## Everything comes together

An offer draws on almost everything earlier in the course. Week four's work holds the line on price, reducing scope instead of discounting. A planned review date is a tactical withdrawal built in before anyone needs it. Second-order thinking stops a vague offer from turning into years of unpaid work. The review is a feedback loop. And principle three, develop your killer instinct, supplies the nerve to ask for the deposit and accept that some people will say no.

## Every response counts

A yes is a commitment to deliver well. A no is information about fit, price or timing. Silence gets one polite follow-up after a few days, and then it is recorded as data too.

Your pass in this course never depends on someone saying yes. It depends on making a clear offer to the people it is for and learning honestly from what happens. And your offer does not have to involve money. A free pilot, an invitation to take part or a release of something you made, with a clear request attached, all count.

## Two examples

A joiner has designed a storage system for small workshops. His first instinct is to post that he can help any local business get organised, which sounds capable and gets no replies. His second idea, sorting a friend's garage workshop for free with no end date, turns into weeks of unpaid Saturdays. His third is a bounded pilot: four weeks, one workshop, one wall system designed and fitted, a fixed fee with half up front, and a review on day twenty-eight. He sends it to six businesses he already knows. Two decline politely, one does not reply, two are interested but not this month, and a motorbike repair shop pays the deposit that week. Count what one yes gives him: a first client, a fitted wall to photograph, and four more businesses who now know exactly what he does.

An illustrator posts that her commissions are open. Six likes, no orders. Nobody knows what to ask for or what it costs. Her new offer: pet portraits in her style, A4 print, two-week turnaround, eighty pounds, five slots this month, send a photo and a deposit to book. Four slots go in two days, and one person asks about A3, which gives her the next offer to test. Same artist, same skill, same audience. The only change was the edges.

## Where people go wrong

**No price.** Making people ask how much. Most will not.

**No edges.** Unlimited scope that turns into resentment.

**No ask.** A lovely description that never says what to do next.

**The hidden offer.** Making it once, quietly, to two people, and concluding nobody wanted it.
