## Most bad deals feel good

Every experienced negotiator knows something that newcomers learn the hard way: most bad deals feel good on the day they are made.

The immediate result is easy to see. They said yes. The sale closed. The awkward silence ended. Everyone shook hands. The cost arrives later, quietly, often after you have forgotten the decision that caused it. The discount that won one sale becomes the price the client expects for ever. The favour becomes an expectation. The easy yes grows a long tail.

This chapter is about seeing that tail before you agree.

## And then what?

The sixth thinking tool is second-order consequences. The question is: **what follows the immediate result?**

First-order thinking asks whether this works today. Second-order thinking asks what it sets in motion. Most people stop at the first order, because that is where the relief is.

The method is almost embarrassingly simple. Ask "and then what?", then ask it again. First order: they say yes and you get the deal. Second order: they now expect the same terms next time. Third order: other clients hear about it and ask for the same, and your prices drop across the board. Three steps is usually enough. Beyond that you are mostly guessing.

## Second-level thinking

Howard Marks, co-founder of the investment firm Oaktree Capital Management, is known for the memos he has written to clients for decades. In *The Most Important Thing* (2011) he gathered their central ideas, and the first of them is what he calls second-level thinking.

First-level thinking, in Marks's account, is simple and superficial: this is a good company, so buy the shares. Second-level thinking is deeper and more demanding. It asks what everyone else already believes, what is already reflected in the price, what range of outcomes is possible, how likely each one is, and what happens if the obvious view turns out to be wrong. Marks argues that because first-level thinking is easy and common, it rarely produces an advantage. The advantage goes to people who think further.

Marks writes about markets, but the habit transfers to any decision with consequences. The obvious answer to "should I give this discount to win the deal?" is yes. The second-level answer asks what that discount does to next year.

## The limit of the tool

Second-order thinking comes with a warning: speculation is not prediction. Every ripple you trace is a guess about the future, and some guesses will be wrong. So label your confidence. "Quite likely" and "possible but unlikely" belong on the same sheet, clearly marked. Then decide. Marks himself is emphatic that the future cannot be known with certainty, and that good decisions depend on thinking in ranges and probabilities, an idea this course returns to in Chapter 16.

## Holding the line

The other half of this chapter is about what to do once you can see the consequences.

Holding the line still leaves room to move. You simply move only in exchange for something. Less scope for less money. A paid pilot for a later commitment. Faster payment for a small discount. This echoes a core idea from *Getting to Yes*, introduced in Chapter 10: invent options for mutual gain instead of haggling over a single number. When you trade instead of conceding, both sides can come away with something they value.

And you prepare all of it before you walk in. A clear request. Two alternatives. And a limit, the point below which you will politely leave. Decide it while you are calm, because nobody makes a good decision with someone waiting across the table.

## Two principles meet

Principle three, develop your killer instinct, appears again. It takes nerve to make a clear request at full price and to be ready for no.

Principle eight, burn the bridge of permission, returns from week one. You do not need the other side's permission to value your own work. You set your price and let them decide. Their decision is theirs. Your price is yours.

## Two examples

The owner of a two-person video studio is close to landing his first corporate client: a series of six videos for a regional housebuilder. In the final meeting their marketing manager asks for thirty per cent off. The first-order answer is to say yes and win. He asks what follows. The discounted rate becomes his rate with this client. Corporate clients talk, and people move jobs, so his first corporate price becomes his reputation. And at that price he cannot afford the second crew member he needs, so the quality drops on the most important work he has ever done. He holds the price and offers two alternatives: four videos instead of six, or one paid pilot at full rate with the rest to follow if it performs. They choose the pilot. It performs. They sign for the other five at full price.

A woman who lets out one flat gets a message from her tenant asking to pay late this month after losing some shifts. The first-order answer is yes, and the conversation ends kindly. The second order is that without a date, late can quietly become normal, and her own mortgage payment leaves her account on the first of each month. The third order is that a few months of drift could end in a crisis or a confrontation, damaging the very relationship she wanted to protect. So she says yes, by the fifteenth, confirmed in a short friendly message. It is still kind. It protects them both. The tenant pays on the fourteenth.

## Where people go wrong

**Paralysis.** Chasing consequences for ever and never deciding.

**Only downside.** Forgetting that second-order effects can be good. A fair price now can win referrals later.

**No limit.** Walking in without a line you have already decided not to cross.

**The fortune teller.** Treating your guesses about the future as certainties and arguing about things that have not happened.
