## The word nobody wants

Few words in English carry as much disapproval as "selfish". Children hear it before they can spell it. It is the charge levelled at the sibling who will not share, the colleague who leaves on time, the friend who moved away for a job. By adulthood most of us have absorbed a simple rule: wanting things for yourself is suspect, and wanting them openly is worse.

The rule does a great deal of good. It keeps families together, it makes workplaces bearable and it reminds us that other people are real. But it has a cost that rarely gets counted. Applied without thought, it teaches people to treat their own futures as the least important item on every list. Their ambitions become something to fit around everyone else's needs, pursued in whatever scraps of time and energy are left once all the requests have been met. For most people, very little is left.

This course begins by putting the word back on the table. Selfishness as a virtue sounds like a provocation, and it is meant to. The provocation forces a question most people never ask directly: does your own flourishing have a legitimate claim on your life, or does it only get what nobody else wants?

## Where the phrase comes from

The phrase is borrowed from Ayn Rand, the Russian-born American novelist and philosopher, who published a collection of essays in 1964 called *The Virtue of Selfishness*. Rand's argument, set out most fully in the essay "The Objectivist Ethics", was that the ordinary use of the word "selfish" had been rigged. People used it to cover two very different things: the thug who takes what he wants by force, and the person who simply pursues their own life with care and reason. Lumping them together, she argued, made the second person feel guilty for the sins of the first.

Rand's own position was that rational self-interest is the proper basis of ethics. A person's own life is their highest value, and the virtues worth having (honesty, independence, productiveness, integrity) are the ones that serve that life over the long run. Crucially, she drew a hard line between self-interest and whim. Doing whatever you feel like in the moment is the opposite of what she meant. Self-interest in her sense requires thought, judgement and a willingness to accept consequences.

She also drew a line between self-interest and taking from others. In her view a person of rational self-interest neither sacrifices themselves to others nor sacrifices others to themselves. Exchange, freely agreed and fairly priced, is how such people deal with one another.

Rand is a contested figure. Many philosophers reject her conclusions, and her wider politics go far beyond anything this course needs. You do not have to become an Objectivist to take the course, and we are not asking you to. What we take from her is narrower and, we think, hard to argue with: the distinction between considered self-interest and mere appetite, and the observation that confusing the two leaves decent people unable to defend their own priorities.

## What we mean by it

Becoming Doom uses the phrase in a precise way. Selfishness as a virtue means deliberate responsibility for your own flourishing. It has three parts.

**You choose.** You decide what you are building, and you say it out loud and write it down. A goal that only lives in your head is easy to cancel. Nobody notices, including you. Writing it down makes it something you can be held to.

**You protect.** You guard the time, money and attention that your choice needs, with the same seriousness you would bring to anything else you valued. People who would never leave their wallet on a café table leave their evenings open to whoever asks first.

**You own it.** You carry the consequences of your choice, good and bad, without passing the blame to anyone else. If the protected evening costs you a social plan, you accept that cost. If the project fails, you learn from it rather than finding someone to resent.

Notice what is absent from the list. None of it involves taking anything from another person. The whole practice is about ceasing to give away, by default, what is already yours.

## Three voices bidding for your life

The most useful tool in this chapter is a simple distinction between three voices. All three will claim to be you, and every hour, every pound and every unit of attention you have is being bid on by one of them.

**Appetite** wants something now. The scroll, the snooze button, the takeaway instead of the plan, the easy win. Appetite is loud and honest about what it wants. It is the voice Rand was careful to separate from self-interest, and most people already know to be wary of it.

**Approval** wants you to be thought well of. It agrees to the favour so nobody is disappointed. It says yes in the meeting and resents it in the car park. It volunteers for the committee, stays late without being asked and laughs at the joke it did not find funny. Approval is quiet and respectable, which is exactly why it is dangerous. Few people feel guilty for being helpful, so approval can consume a life without ever being challenged.

**Interest** asks what you will be glad you built in five years. It weighs consequences, keeps track of promises, values relationships and respects fair exchange. Interest is the slowest of the three voices and the easiest to drown out. It is also the only one reliably on your side over the long run.

In most lives, interest gets whatever appetite and approval leave behind. The work of this course is turning that voice up until it wins the important bids. Following appetite is the opposite of what we mean by selfishness. Following approval is something subtler and more common: self-abandonment that looks, from the outside, like good character.

## Why approval is the bigger danger

It is worth pausing on that last point, because it is where this course differs from much of the advice people hear.

Most self-improvement writing aims at appetite. Get up earlier, scroll less, eat better, stop procrastinating. Those are reasonable targets. But for the kind of person who signs up for a programme like this, appetite is rarely the main leak. Their days leak away to other people's priorities, granted one reasonable request at a time. Laziness has little to do with it.

Ichiro Kishimi and Fumitake Koga's *The Courage to Be Disliked*, built on the psychology of Alfred Adler, makes a related argument that we return to in Chapter 2. Living to satisfy other people's expectations, they suggest, means living someone else's life. The desire for approval feels like kindness, but it hands control of your choices to whoever happens to be watching.

Approval-driven people are often admired. They are reliable, generous and easy to work with. They are also frequently exhausted and quietly resentful, because the generosity was never really chosen. When every request is granted automatically, giving stops being a gift. It turns into a tax.

## Ruthless with leaks, never with people

The second principle in this chapter is P04: hold a vision and be ruthless. It is easy to misread, so it helps to be exact about the target.

Greg McKeown's *Essentialism* (2014) makes a case that fits well here. McKeown argues for the disciplined pursuit of less: deciding what is genuinely essential, eliminating the rest, and building systems that make the essential easy. One of his central observations is that if you do not set your own priorities, someone else will set them for you. Saying yes to everything, in his account, is a way of avoiding the harder work of choosing.

That is the ruthlessness we mean. You are ruthless with the habit, the excuse, the distraction and the failing method that keep eating your resources. You cancel the subscription you never use. You leave the group chat that steals your mornings. You drop the side project you only keep because quitting would feel like admitting something.

People get something else from you: honesty, fair terms and clear communication. A ruthless person in our sense might decline your request, but they will decline it plainly and offer an alternative if one exists. They will never manipulate you, mislead you or treat you as an obstacle. The knife is pointed inwards, at your own leaks.

## Your ego is not your interest

One more distinction sits underneath everything else. Ego and interest often look alike, and they frequently pull in opposite directions.

Winning an argument can damage your interests if it costs you a relationship you needed. Protecting a flattering picture of yourself can stop you learning the skill you lack. Refusing useful criticism can feel strong while it quietly undermines the very thing you are building. Rand herself placed great weight on reason and reality over feeling; whatever else one thinks of her, she would have had no patience for a self-interest that refused to look at evidence.

So when you ask what serves you, ask about the person you will be in five years. The version of you who wants to be right in this conversation is usually the wrong person to consult.

## Two lives, one principle

Consider two people.

The first is a freelance designer. For a year he has wanted to rebuild his portfolio, because it is the only thing likely to bring the clients he actually wants. Every week something gets in the way, usually a reasonable request from an existing client. When the fourth unpaid round of revisions arrives, he has three choices. He can do it that night and let the portfolio slide again. He can refuse flatly and risk the relationship. Or he can agree to the work at his normal rate, with a delivery date that protects his evening. The third option serves his interest, and it is also the most honest, because it tells the client the true cost of the request.

The second is a ward nurse. She wants a senior role that requires a qualification, and she has always said yes to every shift swap because she is good at her job and people rely on her. She makes one change: two evenings a week become fixed for study, and every other swap stays open. She remains generous. She has simply stopped being generous with the one resource her future depended on. It is awkward for a fortnight. Then people adjust.

Neither of them became cold or grasping. Each chose something, protected it and owned the cost.

## Where people go wrong

There are three common ways to misuse this idea.

**The tantrum.** Selfishness becomes permission to do whatever you feel like: cancelling without notice, skipping anything boring and calling it self-care. That is appetite wearing a philosophy as a disguise, and it builds nothing.

**The martyr.** Everything is given away, nobody is ever told to stop, and resentment builds towards everyone who accepted what was offered. This is approval in control. It is by far the more common mistake, and many people who take this course are recovering from it.

**The secret plan.** You decide what matters, protect nothing and tell nobody, so that nothing ever has to change. A plan with no cost and no witness is a daydream.

What we want instead is plain to describe and demanding to do: a written choice, a protected resource and an honest cost.

## What this asks of you

The exercise for this chapter is the one in the video: write the first version of your personal doctrine. One page, four questions.

1. What am I building, and why does it matter to me?
2. Which specific resource will I commit to it this week?
3. What is that resource taken from?
4. Which existing responsibility will I honour or renegotiate to make this work?

If your answer to the third question is "nothing", go back to the second. A priority that costs nothing is still a preference.

## A note on disagreement

This chapter takes a position, and you are free to disagree with it. Plenty of serious traditions, religious and secular, put self-giving at the centre of a good life, and nothing here asks you to abandon them. The claim is narrower. Whatever you believe about duty to others, your own flourishing deserves a real vote, made deliberately, rather than whatever is left over when everyone else has finished.

## Further reading

- **Ayn Rand, *The Virtue of Selfishness* (1964).** The source of the phrase. Start with "The Objectivist Ethics". Read it critically; it is a philosophical argument, not a research finding.
- **Greg McKeown, *Essentialism: The Disciplined Pursuit of Less* (2014).** The most practical guide to the ruthlessness half of this chapter.
- **Ichiro Kishimi and Fumitake Koga, *The Courage to Be Disliked*.** A dialogue-based introduction to Adlerian psychology and the cost of living for approval. The centre of Chapter 2.
