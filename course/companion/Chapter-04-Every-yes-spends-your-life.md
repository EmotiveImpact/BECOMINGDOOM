## Week two begins

The first week of the course was about claiming your life as worth investing in. You wrote a doctrine, said a first no and put a label on trial. The second week asks a harder question: can you trust yourself to execute, week after week, when ordinary life pushes back?

It starts with the most useful idea economics has given to everyday life.

## Time does not arrive empty

We tend to describe favours by their size. It's only two hours. It's a quick call. It won't take long.

Size is the wrong measure. Every hour you have is already holding something, even if that something is rest. When a new commitment arrives, it does not slot into empty space. It pushes something else out. The real price of a yes is whatever it displaces.

Most people run their weeks as though there were a hidden reserve, a spare evening that will appear if they agree now and sort it out later. That reserve does not exist. When you add something, something leaves. The only choice you get is whether you pick what leaves or let it be picked for you.

## Opportunity cost

Economists call this opportunity cost: the value of the best alternative you give up when you make a choice. The idea is usually credited to nineteenth-century Austrian economists, and it sits at the heart of every introductory economics course, because it changes the question from "is this worth doing?" to "is this worth more than the best thing it replaces?"

In this course, opportunity cost is the first of ten thinking tools, and it comes with one question: **what is the best realistic alternative I give up?**

Two words carry the weight. *Best*, because you compare against the most valuable thing you could have done, never the least. Comparing a favour against an evening of scrolling makes every favour look worthwhile. *Realistic*, because you compare against what you would actually have done, never a fantasy version of yourself who works flawlessly every night.

Pricing any yes takes three questions. What exactly am I agreeing to, including the preparation, the travel and the follow-up? Where will it come from: which hours, which energy, which money? And is it worth more than the best thing it replaces? Sometimes the answer is yes, and that is fine. The difference is that it is now a choice you made with the price in view.

## The disciplined pursuit of less

Greg McKeown's *Essentialism* (2014) is, in a sense, a whole book about opportunity cost applied to a life. McKeown, a British-born writer who has worked with leaders in large organisations, argues that most capable people are spread too thin. They say yes to so many good things that they make only a little progress on the few things that matter most.

His remedy is what he calls the disciplined pursuit of less. Recognise that trade-offs are unavoidable. Explore options before committing, but then choose few. Eliminate the non-essential, even when it is attractive. Build routines that make the essential easy to do.

One of McKeown's sharpest observations is that trade-offs happen whether you make them or not. If you refuse to choose, your priorities will still be set: by your inbox, your manager, your friends, or whoever asks loudest. Choosing is simply the decision to set them yourself.

## Owning the price

The principle that sits alongside opportunity cost this week is P09: radical accountability is absolute power.

The phrase echoes *Extreme Ownership* (2015) by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin, two former US Navy SEAL officers who led a task unit in the battle of Ramadi in Iraq. Their book takes lessons from combat into business leadership. Its central argument is that leaders must own everything in their world: the plan, the team's performance, the mistakes and the outcomes. Blaming circumstances or subordinates may feel better, they argue, but it removes the leader's ability to fix anything.

This course takes the core of that idea and applies it to a single life. Radical accountability means owning your controllable decisions, including their price. If you choose to give two hours to a colleague and move your project session to Sunday, you own that choice and its consequences.

We also draw a line Willink and Babin would likely recognise. Accountability never means blaming yourself for things that genuinely were not yours. If your Sunday session falls through because your child is ill, that was outside your control. Pretending otherwise is self-punishment, which helps nobody. Accountability is power because it returns the decisions that were yours to your hands. If every lost evening is somebody else's fault, nothing you do can change it. If you can see which choices were yours, you can make different ones next week.

## Two ledgers

A project accountant with two young children has one protected evening a week for his project. On the afternoon of that evening, a stressed colleague asks for two hours of help with a presentation. He is already tired. If he says yes, the time comes from his project or from his sleep. There is no third pot.

He can pay with sleep, help and then work until one in the morning, which costs him Friday and a short temper with his children at the weekend. He can pay with the project and let it slip again, which, repeated weekly, means it never happens. Or he can name the cost and choose. He offers one hour at four o'clock and keeps his evening. Or he decides this colleague matters enough for the full two hours and deliberately moves his session to Sunday. Either version is fine. What matters is that he chose which cost to bear.

A baker who runs a small shop is offered a contract for forty loaves a day at a wholesale discount. It sounds like growth. She writes the ledger. It means four o'clock starts six days a week, her assistant's hours swallowed by the order, a thin margin, and the new range she has been planning for a year never getting made. She counter-offers twenty loaves three days a week at a fair price. The café accepts. The opportunity was real. So was its cost.

## The limit of the tool

Opportunity cost has a failure mode that catches keen learners. Once they see that every hour has a price, they start to treat rest as waste and every evening off as failure. That is a misuse of the tool.

Do not price every free hour as if it could earn unlimited money. Rest is a legitimate use of time. So is seeing friends, reading for pleasure or doing nothing in particular. Opportunity cost is about honest comparison. It is never a reason to feel guilty for resting.

## Where people go wrong

**The worst-case comparison.** Comparing a yes against the least valuable alternative, so everything looks worth doing.

**Hidden hours.** Forgetting the preparation, travel and recovery, so every commitment looks cheaper than it is.

**Guilt maths.** Quietly adding points for how bad a no would feel. Feelings are worth noticing. They should not set the price.

**The frozen ledger.** Pricing a commitment once and never looking again. Prices change. A yes that made sense in January may still be eating your Thursdays in June.
