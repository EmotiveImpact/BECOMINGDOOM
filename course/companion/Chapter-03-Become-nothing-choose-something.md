## The sentence you can finish in a second

Ask almost anyone to complete the sentence "I am not the kind of person who..." and they will do it instantly. Who sells. Who speaks in public. Who is good with money. Who finishes things. Who writes. Who leads.

The speed is revealing. Nobody reaches for that sentence after careful thought. It has been said so often that it no longer feels like a thought at all. It feels like a fact, as solid as height or eye colour.

This chapter is about those sentences: where they come from, why they are so persuasive, and how to put one of them on trial. It is also about two of the most philosophical principles in the creed, and why they turn out to be intensely practical.

## Labels are made

Labels usually form in three steps. First an event: a presentation that went badly, a failed exam, a comment from a teacher or parent. Second a story: you decide what the event means about you, usually on the spot, usually harshly and with very little evidence. Third a rule: the story hardens into a policy you follow for years without checking it again. "I don't do presentations." "I'm not a numbers person."

Once the rule exists, it protects itself. You avoid the situations that might test it, so it is never tested. And because it is never tested, it never loses. Each year without contrary evidence makes it feel a little more true, even though the only evidence it ever had is one moment from long ago.

The point of this chapter is that you can go back to the first step. You can collect a new event.

## There is no meaning written into you

Principle two of the creed says: accept that there is no inherent meaning. Of all fourteen principles it is the most likely to make people uneasy, so it is worth being careful about what it does and does not ask.

It is a philosophical position, and you are free to weigh it and disagree. Many people taking this course hold a religious faith or another worldview that gives life a meaning beyond what any one person chooses, and nothing here asks them to give that up. The principle is useful even within such a faith, because the question it raises is narrower: was this particular verdict about you written into the universe, or did it come from somewhere much smaller?

The tradition behind the principle runs through the French writer and philosopher Albert Camus. In *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942), Camus described what he called the absurd: the collision between the human longing for meaning and a universe that offers no clear answer. His response was neither despair nor a leap into comforting certainty. It was a kind of defiant engagement. He took the Greek myth of Sisyphus, condemned for ever to push a boulder up a hill only to watch it roll down again, and argued that Sisyphus could still own his task. The essay ends with the famous line, "One must imagine Sisyphus happy."

For our purposes the useful move in Camus is this. If meaning is not handed to you in advance, then neither are the labels. "Not a business person" is not a law of nature. Somebody made it, quite possibly you, and what was made can be examined.

## Choosing something

The second half of the lesson title, choose something, draws on a very different writer.

Viktor Frankl was an Austrian psychiatrist who survived several Nazi concentration camps, including Auschwitz. In *Man's Search for Meaning* (1946) he described life in the camps and what he observed about the people who held on. His conclusion, which became the basis for his approach to therapy, was that a sense of meaning is a primary human need, and that it can be found even in the worst conditions: in work worth doing, in love for another person, and in the attitude a person chooses to take towards suffering they cannot avoid.

Frankl's account is a reminder of how much is at stake in the word "choose". He did not argue that people can choose their circumstances. He argued that, within whatever circumstances they face, people keep a space in which they decide how to respond, and that what they choose to work towards shapes who they become.

Camus and Frankl approach meaning from different directions, and they would not agree on everything. Together they give this course its stance on identity. You are not bound by a verdict handed to you. You are responsible for what you choose to build next.

## Believe in your fucking self

Principle six is the loudest line in the creed: believe in your fucking self. It is also the one most open to bad interpretations, so precision matters.

In this course, belief is a decision to act. It means trusting your capacity to take the next step and learn from what happens. It does not mean feeling certain, and it certainly does not mean being right. Confidence and correctness are different things. People who confuse them make expensive mistakes, because they treat a strong feeling as evidence.

The American psychologist Carol Dweck popularised a related idea in *Mindset* (2006). She distinguished between a fixed mindset, the belief that abilities are set and cannot change much, and a growth mindset, the belief that abilities can be developed through effort, good strategies and help from others. People with a fixed mindset, she argued, tend to avoid challenges that might expose a weakness. People with a growth mindset are more willing to try, fail and improve.

It is worth being honest about the evidence. Later research testing short mindset interventions in schools has found effects that are real in some settings but generally small, and some studies have failed to find them at all. The idea is useful as a way of thinking. It is not a guarantee that believing in growth will make growth happen. That is exactly why this course pairs belief with action and evidence. You believe enough to try. Then you let the result teach you.

## Turning a verdict into an experiment

The practical tool in this chapter is simple. You turn a verdict into an experiment.

A verdict says what you are. An experiment asks what happens when you do one small thing. A verdict cannot be tested. An experiment can, and it produces evidence within days.

Three ways of handling a label make the difference clear.

**Obey it.** You do nothing, and the label is confirmed by default. No evidence proved it; your absence did.

**Hype over it.** You announce that you are a CEO now, buy a domain, design a logo and post about your journey. It feels strong for an evening. It produces nothing anyone could check, and the first quiet week brings the label straight back.

**Test it.** You take one small, real action and write down what happened.

A bookkeeper who believes she is "not a business person" asks a local shop owner for twenty minutes to talk about their bookkeeping problems. No pitch, just questions. She learns that the owner loses two evenings a month to their books and would happily pay to stop. She still does not feel like a business person. She now has evidence that someone wants what she can do, which is far more useful than a feeling.

A warehouse supervisor who froze during a best man's speech nine years ago has avoided every microphone since. His experiment is two minutes at Monday's briefing, with notes. His voice shakes for twenty seconds and then settles. Two people ask questions. Two minutes proves nothing about whether he is a great speaker. It proves that the rule he followed for nine years was wrong about what would happen.

## Where people go wrong

**Too big.** Testing a label by launching a business. If it wobbles, the label claims victory.

**No record.** Doing the action but never writing down what happened, so memory rewrites it to fit the old story.

**Moving goals.** Deciding what counts as success after seeing the result.

**The rigged test.** Choosing an experiment the label is certain to win, such as fifty cold calls when you have never made one. Fear is very good at designing tests it knows you will fail. Choose one you could plausibly pass.

## What comes next

The day after this lesson, you choose the Doom Project, the one thing you will build over the next five weeks. Bring the evidence from your experiment with you. Very often the label you test this week is the one that would have vetoed the project you actually wanted.

## Further reading

- **Albert Camus, *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942).** A short, difficult and beautiful essay on living fully without guaranteed meaning.
- **Viktor Frankl, *Man's Search for Meaning* (1946).** One of the most important books of the twentieth century. Brief, harrowing and humane.
- **Carol Dweck, *Mindset* (2006).** The popular account of fixed and growth mindsets. Read it alongside the later research on its limits.
