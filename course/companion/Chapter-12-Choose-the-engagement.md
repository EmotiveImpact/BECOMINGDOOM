## When the room pushes back

You have mapped interests and prepared your line. This chapter covers what happens when the room pushes back: criticism, poor terms, a flat no, or a conversation going nowhere.

Two kinds of people struggle here. Some avoid every difficult conversation, letting bad terms stand and never asking for what they need. Others treat every conversation as a contest they must win, arguing every point and spending huge energy on battles that would change nothing even if they won them. Both are expensive. The skill is choosing which engagements deserve your energy, and leaving the others cleanly.

## Winning without fighting

*The Art of War*, attributed to the Chinese general and strategist Sun Tzu and probably written more than two thousand years ago, is one of the oldest books on strategy still widely read. Much of its advice has nothing to do with charging into battle. It is about knowing when not to fight.

Several of its themes run through this chapter. The best victory, it suggests, is one achieved without fighting at all. A good commander knows his own strengths and weaknesses and those of the enemy, and chooses ground where he can win. He does not commit forces to a position he cannot hold. Retreat, in this tradition, can be a deliberate move that preserves strength for a better moment.

Generals have always withdrawn from ground they could not hold so they could fight on ground they could. Chess players pull a piece back to save it for a stronger move. Withdrawal, done on purpose, is strategy.

## The tactical withdrawal

Principle eleven of the creed says: master the art of the tactical withdrawal.

A tactical withdrawal is a move chosen deliberately to preserve your resources for a better engagement. Running away is driven by discomfort. A tactical withdrawal is driven by a decision rule you made before the pressure started. You leave on purpose, with your reasons clear and your relationships intact.

## Quitting is a skill

Annie Duke, a former professional poker player who now writes about decision-making, argues in *Quit* (2022) that our culture celebrates persistence so heavily that we are bad at knowing when to stop. Most people, she suggests, quit too late: they stay in jobs, projects, relationships and investments long after the evidence says go, held there by sunk costs, by fear of being seen as a quitter and by the hope that things will turn around.

One of her most useful recommendations is to set what she calls kill criteria in advance: specific conditions that, if they occur, mean you will stop. She illustrates the idea with mountaineers on Everest, who set a fixed turnaround time. If they have not reached the summit by that hour, they turn back, however close they are, because the decision has to be made before exhaustion and summit fever cloud their judgement.

That is the decision rule this chapter asks you to write.

## Your walk-away point

*Getting to Yes*, introduced in Chapter 10, adds a final piece. Fisher and Ury argue that every negotiator should know their BATNA: their best alternative to a negotiated agreement. If the deal on the table is worse than your best alternative, you should walk away. If you have no alternatives, you are in a weak position and should work on creating some before you negotiate.

Knowing your alternatives is what makes a calm exit possible. People who feel trapped in one negotiation tend either to cave or to explode. People with options can simply leave.

## Sort what you heard

Before you decide whether to stay or go, sort what you heard into three piles.

**Rejection**: a no. It tells you about fit, timing or budget. It says nothing about your worth, and it does not need to be argued with. **Criticism**: something specific you can act on. This is the most valuable pile, even when it stings. **Uncertainty**: they have not decided. That calls for one clear follow-up at a sensible time, never a flood of chasing messages.

Most people lump all three into one feeling, usually "it went badly". Sorting them turns a feeling into a plan.

## Continue, change or withdraw

Write your decision rule before you enter the room.

**Continue** if the terms stay within your limits and progress is real. **Change** if the goal is still sound but the approach is failing: a different person, a different offer, a different timescale. **Withdraw** if a limit is crossed or the cost of continuing now outweighs the prize.

Bring this week's other tools to it. Incentives: is the other side rewarded for something that means they can never give you what you need? Second-order consequences: if you stay, what does that set in motion?

Under pressure you will not invent a good rule. You will only follow one you already have.

## A clean exit

A clean exit has three parts. **The limit**: name plainly what does not work for you. **The door**: say what would bring you back, if anything would. **The thanks**: leave with the relationship intact, because industries are small, people move jobs and everyone remembers how you left.

## Two examples

A founder launching a small skincare range has negotiated with a manufacturer for two weeks, and each round the terms get worse: slower payment, a higher minimum order, a shorter guarantee. In the third round they fall below her limits. She could cave because she is tired. She could turn it into a contest and burn a bridge in a small industry. Instead she says the terms do not work for her, that she is ready to sign this week if they return to the original minimum and guarantee, and thanks them for their time. Two days later they call back with the original terms. Had they not, she had already shortlisted two alternatives.

A volunteer on a rugby club committee watches the same two people reopen a settled argument for an hour every month. Arguing back only makes it longer. He sets a rule: if a decided item is reopened without new facts, he will say so once and step out for that part of the meeting. He tells the chair in advance, then does it twice. By the third meeting, the chair closes the item before it starts. Withdrawal changed the room more than winning the argument would have.

## Where people go wrong

**Leaving hot.** Walking out angry. The last line is the one they remember.

**Ghosting.** Disappearing instead of closing the conversation properly.

**Quitting early.** Calling discomfort a tactical withdrawal. Some conversations are meant to be uncomfortable. Check your decision rule, never your mood.

**Never leaving.** Persistence without a rule becomes stubbornness.

## Further reading

- **Sun Tzu, *The Art of War*.** Short and endlessly reinterpreted. Read a modern translation with notes.
- **Annie Duke, *Quit* (2022).** A clear, evidence-informed case for quitting well, including kill criteria.
- **Roger Fisher and William Ury, *Getting to Yes* (1981).** Revisit the chapter on your best alternative to a negotiated agreement.
