## Slide 1 | title

**On screen:** PRESERVE ROOM TO RECOVER.
**Visual:** hood

**Say:**

Doomsday is close. In a few days you will stand up and demonstrate your project, defend a decision and respond to a change you did not see coming.

And on the day, something will probably not go to plan. This lesson makes sure that when it goes wrong, you can recover.

## Slide 2 | scene

**On screen:** THE WIFI DROPS.
**Visual:** scene: a laptop on a lectern in a community hall showing a "No internet connection" page, a projector beam lighting dust in the air

**Say:**

The wifi drops. The tool you rely on is down. The file will not open on the projector. The one person who was going to help you cannot make it. Your train is cancelled.

## Slide 3 | question

**On screen:** What happens when it goes wrong?

**Say:**

None of this is unusual. Every one of these happens to someone, somewhere, every day.

What matters is whether you planned for it. Two people can face exactly the same setback. One loses a morning. The other loses everything. The difference is almost always decided before the setback arrives.

## Slide 4 | scene

**On screen:** BUILT TO CARRY MORE THAN THE LOAD.
**Visual:** scene: the underside of a large steel railway bridge, a train crossing overhead, massive rivets and girders lit in the red of sunset

**Say:**

Engineers build bridges to carry far more than the heaviest load they expect. Not because they think the bridge will be overloaded, but because they know their estimates might be wrong, materials vary, and the cost of being wrong is a bridge in a river.

That extra capacity is called a margin of safety. You build plans the same way.

## Slide 5 | red

**On screen:** WHAT BUFFER OR FALLBACK KEEPS A PLAUSIBLE SETBACK MANAGEABLE?

**Say:**

The tenth and final thinking tool is margin of safety. The question: what buffer or fallback keeps a plausible setback manageable?

Two words matter. Plausible: you are preparing for things that could reasonably happen, not every disaster you can imagine. And manageable: the goal is to keep the setback small enough to deal with, not to make it impossible.

## Slide 6 | scene

**On screen:** LIKELY. COSTLY. AFFORDABLE.
**Visual:** scene: a spare tyre, a torch and a folded hi-vis vest in the boot of a car, neatly packed

**Say:**

How much margin is enough? Ask three questions.

## Slide 7 | list

**On screen:** HOW MUCH MARGIN?

**Say:**

Is the setback likely? Buffer what happens often. Late trains, flat batteries, tired evenings, software that needs updating at the worst moment.

Is it costly? Buffer what would end the project, even if it is rare. A lost laptop with no backup. A single supplier with no alternative.

Is the buffer affordable? Never spend more protecting against a risk than the risk itself would cost you. A spare tyre makes sense. A spare car does not.

This is week three's inversion, grown up. Inversion asks what would make it fail. Margin of safety asks how much room you need to recover when it does.

## Slide 8 | scene

**On screen:** TIME. RESOURCES. FALLBACK.
**Visual:** scene: a memory stick, a printed one-page summary and a phone with a hotspot icon, laid out on a desk the night before a presentation

**Say:**

There are three kinds of margin, and you will usually want a little of each.

Time. Plan to finish early enough to absorb one bad day. If Doomsday is Thursday, aim to be ready by Tuesday.

Resources. Keep a local copy of anything that lives online. A spare charger. A printed version of anything that matters. Money set aside, if money is involved.

Fallback. Prepare a smaller version of your demonstration that still shows what matters. If the full thing fails, you can show the core in five minutes.

## Slide 9 | story

**On screen:** The service went down ten minutes before he presented.

**Say:**

Here is Arif. His Doom Project is a small web tool for community gardens. Volunteers use it to sign up for watering shifts, and the coordinators can see at a glance which plots need help. Three gardens have been using it for a fortnight.

The night before his demonstration, he runs his margin check. Is there a copy that runs on his laptop without the internet? Yes, he made one last week. Is there a smaller demo if the full one fails? He writes a five-minute version that shows only the sign-up and the coordinator's view. Has he got screenshots of real use from the three gardens? He saves twelve.

The next morning, ten minutes before he presents, the hosting service the tool depends on goes down.

## Slide 10 | scene

**On screen:** NO MARGIN. TOO MUCH MARGIN. THE RIGHT MARGIN.
**Visual:** scene (Arif at the front of a small room, laptop open, calmly switching to a local copy while the audience settles)

**Say:**

There are three ways that morning could have gone.

## Slide 11 | compare

**On screen:** Three ways the morning could go

**Say:**

No margin. He spends his slot refreshing the page and apologising. He shows nothing. Everything he built in six weeks is invisible because of something that was not his fault.

Margin for everything. In the final week he panicked and built five backup plans: a second hosting provider, a mobile version, a video recording, a slide deck and a paper prototype. He never finished the coordinator view, the main feature. That is the limit on this tool. Buffers have costs, and they cannot remove every risk. Too much margin eats the work it was meant to protect.

Proportionate margin. He tells the room the service is down, opens the local copy and shows the core of the tool in five minutes, then the screenshots of real use. He is calm, because he had already decided what he would do.

And here is the thing. Responding well to a changed constraint is exactly what Doomsday assesses. His setback became his best evidence.

## Slide 12 | scene

**On screen:** THE VAN BROKE DOWN.
**Visual:** scene: a white plumber's van on a residential street with its bonnet up, a man in work clothes on the phone beside it, rain on the windscreen

**Say:**

A second case, from ordinary working life.

Kieran is a self-employed plumber. Last March his van broke down. It took a week to fix. He lost a week of jobs, paid the garage on a credit card, and two regular clients found someone else in the meantime.

## Slide 13 | story

**On screen:** Same breakdown. One day lost.

**Say:**

This year he built a margin. He keeps one month's business costs in a separate account that he does not touch for anything else. And he has an arrangement with a friend, another tradesman, who will lend his spare van at short notice in exchange for the same favour.

In October the van breaks down again. Same garage, same kind of fault.

He borrows his friend's van the next morning, pays the garage from the buffer and loses one day. He keeps every client. The setback was identical. The damage was a fraction of last time, because he prepared when things were calm.

## Slide 14 | scene

**On screen:** RETREAT TO THE SMALLER VERSION.
**Visual:** scene: a man on a stage holding a single printed page, the screen behind him blank, the audience leaning forward

**Say:**

Two principles return here, for the last time before Doomsday.

## Slide 15 | red

**On screen:** A PLANNED RETREAT IS A MARGIN.

**Say:**

Principle eleven, master the art of the tactical withdrawal. A fallback is a planned retreat. When Arif's full demo failed, he withdrew to the smaller version, deliberately, without drama, and kept fighting on ground he could hold.

And principle fourteen, embrace your finite nature. You cannot prepare for everything, because your time is finite. Margin of safety is the discipline of choosing which few setbacks deserve your preparation, and accepting the rest. Hope is not a plan. Endless preparation is not a plan either.

## Slide 16 | scene

**On screen:** MARGIN MISTAKES.
**Visual:** scene: an empty savings jar on a kitchen shelf with a note taped to it reading "EMERGENCIES ONLY", a takeaway bag beside it

**Say:**

Here is where people go wrong.

## Slide 17 | list

**On screen:** ZERO SLACK. ENDLESS PREP. SPENT BUFFER.

**Say:**

Zero slack. A plan that only works if nothing goes wrong. Every minute accounted for, every pound spent, no fallback. It looks efficient. It is fragile.

Endless preparation. So many backups that the actual work never gets done. Preparation can become a way of avoiding the scary bit.

Spent buffer. Using the margin on ordinary days, so it is gone when the bad day comes. The emergency fund pays for a takeaway. The spare day before Doomsday gets filled with a new feature.

And the secret buffer. Keeping a fallback in your head instead of writing it down and testing it. A backup you have never opened is a hope, not a margin. Open the local copy. Run the small demo once.

## Slide 18 | scene

**On screen:** EXPECT THE BAD DAY.
**Visual:** scene: a man calmly packing a laptop bag at night, a checklist on the table beside him with every line ticked

**Say:**

Three things to keep.

Expect the bad day. Buffer what is likely or what would be fatal, and accept the rest. And keep the buffer for emergencies, never for ordinary Tuesdays.

## Slide 19 | steps

**On screen:** BUILD YOUR CONTINGENCY SHEET.

**Say:**

Pause the video when I tell you to.

One. List the three most likely setbacks for your Doomsday demonstration. Use inversion: it is Doomsday and it went wrong because...

Two. Give each one a buffer or a fallback. Time, resources or a smaller version.

Three. Write what each buffer costs you, in time, money or effort.

Four. Cut any buffer that costs more than the risk it covers. Then test the one you kept that matters most. Open the backup. Run the short version once, out loud, with a timer.

Pause now and give it ten minutes.
