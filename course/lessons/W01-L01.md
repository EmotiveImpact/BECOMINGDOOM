# W01-L01: Selfishness as a virtue

## Slide 1 | title

**On screen:** SELFISHNESS AS A VIRTUE.
**Visual:** b-open (the hooded figure, face visible, red light bar across the eyes)

**Say:**

Welcome to Becoming Doom. Six weeks, one project, and a version of you that has stopped asking for permission to want things.

This first lesson carries the idea the whole course stands on. Selfishness as a virtue.

If that phrase makes you a little uncomfortable, good. Stay with the discomfort for ten minutes. By the end of this lesson you will know exactly what we mean by it, what we refuse to mean by it, and you will have written the first page of your own doctrine.

## Slide 2 | scene

**On screen:** THE PLAN. THE NOTEBOOK. STILL CLOSED.
**Visual:** scene: a man at a kitchen table late at night, laptop open on someone else's work, a closed notebook with his own plans pushed to the edge of the table

**Say:**

Think of the thing you keep saying matters. The business. The book. The body. The skill. The move to another city.

You have probably said it for a while. You may have said it for years. And there is a good chance that, right now, it sits somewhere like that notebook: close enough to see, never quite open.

## Slide 3 | question

**On screen:** What has it actually been allowed to cost?

**Say:**

So answer one question honestly. In the last month, what has it actually been allowed to cost?

Which evening did it win? Which request did it beat? Which comfortable habit did it replace?

If the honest answer is nothing, then right now it is a preference. A wish with good manners. A priority has to be allowed to take something that something else wanted. Until it does, it stays in the notebook.

## Slide 4 | image

**On screen:** DOOM DOES NOT WAIT TO BE CHOSEN.
**Visual:** b-throne (the hooded figure seated, red light, empty room)

**Say:**

Our emblem is Doom. In the stories he is a man who decided what he would become and then built the capability to become it. He did not wait for a vote. He did not check whether his ambition was acceptable to the room.

We take one trait from him to start: authorship. He treats his own life as something to be built on purpose.

We also watch where he goes wrong, because the stories are honest about that too. He confuses his own judgement with a right to rule other people. We leave that part behind. Your ambition gives you a claim on your own resources. It gives you no claim on anyone else's.

## Slide 5 | red

**On screen:** PUT YOURSELF ABOVE ALL PEOPLE.

**Say:**

Here is the proposition. Your life is a legitimate object of your own ambition.

Your future has a real claim on today's time, attention, money and effort. You do not need to make your ambition inoffensive before you are allowed to pursue it.

Principle one of our creed says: put yourself above all people. Read it precisely. It means your flourishing gets a real vote in how your life is spent, including on the days when someone would prefer it did not.

Other people still matter. You simply stop being the one person in your life whose needs are always negotiable.

## Slide 6 | scene

**On screen:** THE LAST PERSON ON THE LIST.
**Visual:** scene: a woman in a busy shared office, three colleagues leaning over her desk with papers, her own screen minimised in the corner

**Say:**

Most people were raised to put themselves last and call it character. Help first. Ask later, or never. Be easy to work with. Be the one who can always be relied on.

Some of that is genuinely good. Reliability is a strength. Generosity is a strength. The trouble starts when it stops being a choice. When every request is granted automatically, generosity has quietly turned into being spent.

## Slide 7 | list

**On screen:** WHAT WE MEAN BY SELFISH.

**Say:**

So this is what we mean by selfishness as a virtue. Deliberate responsibility for your own flourishing, in three parts.

You choose. You decide what you are building, and you say it out loud and write it down. A goal that only lives in your head can be quietly cancelled whenever it becomes inconvenient.

You protect. You guard the time, the money and the attention that it needs, the same way you would guard anything else you valued. You would not leave your wallet open on a train table. Most people leave their evenings exactly like that.

And you own it. You carry the consequences of that choice, good and bad, without handing the blame to anyone else.

Look at what is missing from the list. Nothing on it asks you to take from another person. It asks you to stop giving away what is yours by default.

## Slide 8 | poster

**On screen:** WHO IS SPENDING YOUR LIFE?
**Visual:** b-command (the hooded figure standing, red light, city at night behind glass)

**Say:**

To do this properly you need to tell apart three voices, because all three will claim to be you.

Every hour you have, every pound and every bit of attention is being bid on. The question for the rest of this course is simple. Which voice wins the bid?

## Slide 9 | dossier

**On screen:** APPETITE. APPROVAL. INTEREST.

**Say:**

The first voice is appetite. Appetite wants something now. The scroll, the extra hour in bed, the easy win, the takeaway instead of the plan. It is loud and it is honest about what it wants. It is not your interest.

The second voice is approval. Approval wants you to be thought well of. It says yes to the favour so nobody is disappointed. It agrees in the meeting and resents it in the car park. It is quieter than appetite and far more respectable, which is why, for most people, it eats much more of their life.

The third voice is interest. Interest asks what you will be glad you built in five years. It weighs consequences. It counts your relationships, the promises you have made and the value of a fair exchange. Interest is the slowest of the three voices, and the only one that is on your side over the long run.

In most lives interest gets the scraps. Selfishness as a virtue means turning that voice up until it wins the important bids. Obeying appetite is the opposite of it. Obeying approval is self-abandonment with a polite face.

## Slide 10 | scene

**On screen:** FOURTH UNPAID REVISION. PORTFOLIO: STILL CLOSED.
**Visual:** b-scene (Daniel at his desk, client email on screen, portfolio folder untouched)

**Say:**

Let me make this real.

Daniel is a freelance designer. For a year he has wanted to rebuild his portfolio, because it is the only thing that will get him the kind of clients he actually wants. Thursday evening is the time he set aside for it.

On Thursday afternoon an existing client asks for another round of changes. It is the fourth round. It was never in the agreement. They are pleasant about it, and they need it by the morning.

## Slide 11 | compare

**On screen:** Three ways to answer the same email

**Say:**

He has three ways to answer.

The automatic yes. He does the revision that night. The client is happy. The portfolio stays closed for the fourth week running, and he quietly resents a client who did nothing worse than ask. That decision was made by approval.

The flat refusal. That's outside our agreement, so I can't do it. His evening is protected, which is fair. Yet he has not stopped to ask whether this client matters to his future, and he may have spent goodwill he wanted to keep.

The renegotiated scope. Happy to do another round. It's outside the original brief, so it's billed at my day rate, and you'll have it on Monday afternoon. He keeps the client, he prices his time, and he spends Thursday on the portfolio.

The third answer is the selfish one, in our sense. It serves his interest. It is also the fairest of the three, because it tells the client the truth about the cost. The automatic yes hid that cost and let resentment collect it later.

## Slide 12 | scene

**On screen:** TUESDAY. THURSDAY. FIXED.
**Visual:** scene: a nurse in scrubs at the end of a shift, writing on a staff rota board in a hospital corridor, two dates circled in red

**Say:**

A second case, because this is for more than people who run their own business.

Amara is a ward nurse. She wants the senior role, and the senior role needs a qualification. Every week someone asks her to swap a shift, and she has always said yes, because she is good at her job and people rely on her.

## Slide 13 | story

**On screen:** Generous with everything except the one thing her future needed.

**Say:**

So she makes one change. She blocks two evenings, Tuesday and Thursday, for study. She tells the rota coordinator those two are fixed. Every other swap stays open.

Notice what she kept. She is still generous. She still covers for people. She has simply stopped being generous with the one thing her future depended on.

It was awkward for about two weeks. One colleague made a comment. Then people adjusted, the way people almost always do. Twelve weeks later the qualification was done, and one of the colleagues who had grumbled asked her how she had managed it.

That is what deliberate responsibility tends to look like from the outside. A little friction at first. Then respect.

## Slide 14 | poster

**On screen:** YOUR EGO IS NOT YOUR INTEREST.
**Visual:** b-panther (black panther in halftone, red eye light)

**Say:**

One warning before you build anything.

Your ego and your interest are two different things, and they will often pull in opposite directions. Winning an argument can damage your interests. Protecting a flattering picture of yourself can stop you learning. Refusing a useful criticism can feel strong while it quietly wrecks the thing you are building.

When you ask what serves you, ask about the person you will be in five years, and leave the person who wants to be right in this conversation out of it.

## Slide 15 | red

**On screen:** HOLD A VISION. BE RUTHLESS.

**Say:**

Principle four says: hold a vision and be ruthless.

The ruthlessness is about focus. You cut the habit, the excuse, the distraction and the failing method that keep eating your resources. You stop the subscription you never use. You leave the group chat that steals your mornings. You drop the side project you only keep because quitting it would feel like admitting something.

Point the ruthlessness at your own leaks. People get your honesty, your fairness and your clear terms. Your weaknesses get the knife.

## Slide 16 | scene

**On screen:** SELFISH, BADLY.
**Visual:** scene: a man on a sofa at midday, phone in hand, blinds half closed, unopened post on the floor

**Say:**

There are two common ways to get this wrong, and you will recognise at least one of them.

## Slide 17 | list

**On screen:** THE TANTRUM. THE MARTYR.

**Say:**

The first is the tantrum. You hear selfishness as a virtue and decide it means doing whatever you feel like. You cancel things without telling anyone, you skip what bores you and you call it self-care. That is appetite with a philosophy attached, and it builds nothing.

The second is the martyr. You give everything away. Nobody ever asked you to stop, so you never did, and now you quietly resent every person who took what you offered. That is approval in charge, and it is by far the more common mistake. Most people who take this course are recovering martyrs.

A third, quieter mistake sits between them: the secret plan. You decide what matters, you protect nothing and you tell nobody, so nothing ever has to change. A plan with no cost and no witness is a daydream.

What we want is simple to describe and hard to do. A written choice. A protected resource. An honest cost. That combination is your doctrine, and you are about to write it.

## Slide 18 | scene

**On screen:** CHOOSE. PROTECT. OWN.
**Visual:** scene: hands writing in a notebook under a single desk lamp, a phone face down beside it

**Say:**

Before you write, keep three things.

A priority has to be allowed to cost something. Serve your interest, and give neither appetite nor approval the final vote. And be ruthless with your own leaks, never with people.

## Slide 19 | steps

**On screen:** WRITE YOUR DOCTRINE.

**Say:**

Now we build. You are going to write the first version of your personal doctrine. It fits on one page, and it answers four questions.

One. What am I building, and why does it matter to me? Write the reason that is true for you, even if it would impress nobody.

Two. Which resource will I commit to it this week? Make it specific. Five mornings, six until seven. Two hundred pounds. Saturday afternoons from one o'clock.

Three. What is that resource taken from? Name the real cost. Television, sleep, a social habit, a paid shift. If it costs nothing, go back to step two, because you have not committed anything yet.

Four. Which existing responsibility will I honour or renegotiate to make this work? Your children, your job, your partner, your rent. Deal with your commitments in the open, so they stop quietly cancelling your plans. Nobody gets abandoned.

Pause the video now and write the first draft. Give it five minutes. Messy is fine.

## Slide 20 | poster

**On screen:** WHERE DID APPROVAL WRITE A LINE?
**Visual:** b-notebook (the doctrine page under red light, one line struck through)

**Say:**

Now read it back and check three things.

Where did approval write a line? Look for anything you included because it sounds good to other people.

Where did appetite write a line? Look for anything that is really a reward dressed up as a priority.

And is there a real cost? If your doctrine asks nothing of you, it will change nothing for you. Fix at least one line. Pause again if you need to.

## Slide 21 | mission

**On screen:** Today's mission: W01-D02

**Say:**

Your mission today is workbook page W01-D02.

Finish doctrine version one. Put the first protected session in your calendar today, with a start and a finish time, and write down what it is taken from.

That is your evidence. Nobody needs to approve it. Nobody needs to hear your reasons. It only needs to be true.

## Slide 22 | close

**On screen:** YOUR AMBITION DESERVES MORE THAN BELIEF.
**Visual:** b-close (the hooded figure walking away down a red-lit corridor)

**Say:**

Tomorrow, before you do anything else, explain self-interest out loud without confusing it with impulse. No notes.

Then we take your doctrine into contact with the first person who wants your protected time. Lesson two: the courage to be disliked.

Your ambition deserves more than your belief. Give it work it can stand on.
