## Slide 1 | title

**On screen:** THINK IN UNCERTAINTY.
**Visual:** hood

**Say:**

Welcome to the final week: become dangerous.

Dangerous has a precise meaning in this course. You keep your judgement when circumstances change, and you act without needing anyone to prompt you. No more weekly instructions. No more being told which tool to use. By Doomsday you choose.

We begin with how to think when you do not know for sure. Which, if you are honest, is most of the time.

## Slide 2 | scene

**On screen:** ONE CLIENT LOVED IT.
**Visual:** scene: a man at a kitchen table reading a long, warm thank-you message on his phone, smiling, a pile of exercise books beside him

**Say:**

One enthusiastic customer can feel like proof. Somebody loved it, they told their friends, they sent a glowing message. Surely others will feel the same.

## Slide 3 | question

**On screen:** Is it proof, or is it a signal?

**Say:**

It is evidence. It is good evidence of interest, and you should be pleased about it.

It is also a long way from proof of a dependable market. One person is one person. The question is how far that one data point should move you, and what you would need to see next.

## Slide 4 | scene

**On screen:** THE WEATHER FORECAST NEVER SAYS "YES".
**Visual:** scene: a weather map on a television in a café, a large "70%" over a rain cloud, a man checking it while putting on his coat

**Say:**

Weather forecasters do not say it will rain or it will not. They say there is a seventy per cent chance of rain. And over thousands of forecasts, a good forecaster's seventy per cent days really do end up wet about seven times in ten.

You do not need their data or their computers. You need their habit: holding beliefs with a degree of confidence, instead of as yes or no.

## Slide 5 | red

**On screen:** WHAT OUTCOMES ARE PLAUSIBLE, AND WHAT EVIDENCE WOULD UPDATE MY CONFIDENCE?

**Say:**

The ninth thinking tool is probabilistic thinking. The question: what outcomes are plausible, and what evidence would update my confidence?

You stop asking whether something is true or false, and start asking how confident you should be, and why. And you decide, in advance, what would change your mind.

## Slide 6 | scene

**On screen:** RANGES. BASE RATES. UPDATES.
**Visual:** scene: a market trader at dawn checking a small notebook of past takings by month, a flask of tea beside her

**Say:**

Three habits make you better at this.

## Slide 7 | list

**On screen:** THREE HABITS OF CALIBRATED PEOPLE.

**Say:**

Ranges. Say likely, unlikely or uncertain, instead of a flat yes or no. "This will work" becomes "this will probably work for some people, and I am unsure how many".

Base rates. Before you think about your special case, ask how often this kind of thing usually works for people in your position. How many new tutors fill their week in the first term? How many market stalls survive their first winter? You may beat the base rate. Start from it anyway.

Updates. Decide in advance what evidence would change your mind, so you notice it when it arrives. Without this, people only notice the evidence that agrees with them.

## Slide 8 | scene

**On screen:** LOW. MEDIUM. HIGH.
**Visual:** scene: a confidence statement handwritten on an index card, the words LOW, MEDIUM and HIGH each followed by a short condition

**Say:**

Write your confidence in plain words. Low, medium or high. Then say what would move it.

For example. Low today: one keen client. Medium if three of the next ten people I speak to book a trial without me chasing them. High if two of them come back and pay full price for a second month.

The limit on this tool: numbers without a basis are false precision. Saying "seventy-three per cent" sounds rigorous. Unless you can say where that number came from, it means nothing, and it can make a guess look like a fact. Plain words are more honest than invented numbers.

## Slide 9 | story

**On screen:** One glowing client. Resign next month?

**Say:**

Here is Paul. He taught maths in a secondary school for twenty years. He is good at it, and exhausted by everything around it. His Doom Project is a private tutoring practice for students preparing for their GCSEs.

His first client, the daughter of a friend of a friend, went from a grade four to a grade six in her mock exams. Her mother has told everyone. Paul has had a thank-you card, two enquiries and a lot of encouragement.

He is tempted to hand in his notice next month.

## Slide 10 | scene

**On screen:** CERTAIN SUCCESS. CERTAIN FAILURE. OR UPDATE.
**Visual:** scene (Paul at his desk in a quiet classroom after the last bell, a resignation letter drafted on his laptop, a thank-you card propped against the screen)

**Say:**

He has three ways to read what has happened.

## Slide 11 | compare

**On screen:** Three ways to read one success

**Say:**

Certain success. He resigns on one data point. No savings buffer, no plan for the summer when exam students disappear, no idea how many enquiries turn into regular clients. If it works, he was lucky. If it does not, he is out of a job and out of savings.

Certain failure. He decides it was luck, a bright student who would have improved anyway. He never tests it again, and stays in a job that is wearing him down.

Update. He writes a confidence statement. Confidence that tutoring could replace his salary: low to medium. Evidence: one strong result, two enquiries, a known need in his area. He decides he will revisit the resignation if five regular paying clients have booked by the end of March, and if at least three of them came from people he did not already know.

Until then he keeps teaching, takes the two enquiries, and builds the evidence.

## Slide 12 | scene

**On screen:** THE BASE RATE.
**Visual:** scene: a quiet town market in January, half the stalls empty under grey light, one stall still trading with steam rising from a pot

**Say:**

A second case.

Ngozi sells her own West African sauces at a weekend market. She sold out two summer Saturdays in a row, and a café owner asked if she would supply them. Now she wants to sign a twelve-month lease on a small shop.

## Slide 13 | story

**On screen:** Summer is busy for everyone. Winter is the test.

**Say:**

She checks the base rate first. She asks three other traders she trusts how their takings change through the year. All three say the same thing. Summer markets are busy for everyone. January and February are the real test.

So her two sell-outs tell her something, but less than she had hoped. Her confidence that a shop would pay: medium, maybe low.

Her update rule: keep the stall going through January and February. If takings stay above half of the summer level, and the café order continues, she signs a lease in March. If not, she looks at a smaller step, like a shared kitchen or a second market.

She keeps the ambition and drops the gamble. That is the difference between a vision and a bet.

## Slide 14 | scene

**On screen:** WHAT WOULD CHANGE YOUR MIND?
**Visual:** scene: a man sitting alone on a park bench in winter, a notebook open on his knee, pen paused

**Say:**

The most useful question in this whole lesson is this one. What evidence would change your mind?

If the honest answer is nothing, then you are holding a belief rather than making a judgement. That is fine for your values. You do not need evidence to believe that honesty matters. It is dangerous for your plans, because plans meet reality, and reality does not care what you believe.

## Slide 15 | red

**On screen:** YOUR TIME IS FINITE. BET IT WISELY.

**Say:**

Two principles meet here.

Principle fourteen says: embrace your finite nature. Your time is limited. That is exactly why you cannot afford to bet it all on one data point, and exactly why you cannot afford to waste it ignoring a real signal either. Calibration is how a finite person spends time well.

And principle four comes back: hold a vision and be ruthless. Paul keeps the vision of his own practice. Ngozi keeps the vision of her shop. Ruthlessness here means being ruthless with your own wishful thinking, while the vision stays fixed. Hold the vision firmly and the evidence honestly.

## Slide 16 | scene

**On screen:** FALSE CONFIDENCE.
**Visual:** scene: a slick presentation slide on a big screen showing "87% likely to succeed" in huge type, an audience applauding

**Say:**

Here is where people go wrong.

## Slide 17 | list

**On screen:** FAKE PRECISION. STORY OVER DATA. NEVER UPDATING.

**Say:**

Fake precision. Eighty-seven per cent sounds rigorous. Ask where it came from. If nobody can tell you, it is a feeling with a percentage sign.

Story over data. One vivid success outweighs many quiet failures in your head. The client who loved it is a story. The nine who did not reply are data.

Never updating. Treating your first estimate as a promise you have to defend. Changing your mind when the evidence changes is a strength. It is the whole point.

And always updating. Some people swing from certain success to certain failure with every new piece of news. One bad week is one bad week. Move your confidence a step at a time, in proportion to the evidence.

## Slide 18 | scene

**On screen:** SPEAK IN RANGES.
**Visual:** scene: a man and a woman talking across a kitchen table over coffee, one holding up a hand flat and tilting it side to side, the gesture for "maybe"

**Say:**

Three things to keep.

Speak in ranges, using plain words. Check the base rate before your special case. And know in advance what would change your mind.

## Slide 19 | steps

**On screen:** STATE YOUR CONFIDENCE.

**Say:**

Pause the video when I tell you to. From this week, I will tell you less about which tools to use. Today the tool is chosen for you, for the last time.

One. Name the biggest remaining unknown in your Doom Project. The thing you are least sure about.

Two. State your confidence in plain words. Low, medium or high.

Three. List the evidence behind that confidence. What have you actually seen? Include the base rate if you can find one.

Four. Write what would move it up, and what would move it down. Then choose one useful check you can run before Doomsday.

Pause now and give it ten minutes.
