## Slide 1 | title

**On screen:** CHOOSE THE ENGAGEMENT.
**Visual:** hood

**Say:**

You have mapped interests and you have prepared your line. By the time you watch this, you may already have held the conversation.

This lesson covers what happens when the room pushes back. Criticism. Poor terms. A no. A conversation that is going nowhere. The skill is choosing which engagements deserve your energy, and leaving the others cleanly.

## Slide 2 | scene

**On screen:** SOME PEOPLE AVOID EVERY FIGHT.
**Visual:** scene: a man sitting in his car outside an office, engine running, phone in hand, not getting out

**Say:**

There are two kinds of people who struggle here.

Some avoid every difficult conversation. They let bad terms stand, they put up with meetings that go nowhere, and they never ask for what they need, because asking might start a fight.

## Slide 3 | question

**On screen:** Which fights are worth it?

**Say:**

Others treat every conversation as a contest they must win. They argue every point, they cannot let a comment go, and they spend huge amounts of energy on battles that would not change anything if they won.

Both are expensive. Avoiding everything costs you the things you never asked for. Fighting everything costs you time, reputation and relationships. The real question is which engagements are worth your energy.

## Slide 4 | scene

**On screen:** RETREAT ON PURPOSE.
**Visual:** scene: a chessboard mid-game, a hand moving a piece backwards, away from an attack

**Say:**

Good strategists have always known this. Generals withdraw from ground they cannot hold, so they can fight on ground they can. Chess players retreat a piece to save it for a stronger move. Retreat, done deliberately, is a move.

## Slide 5 | red

**On screen:** MASTER THE ART OF THE TACTICAL WITHDRAWAL.

**Say:**

Principle eleven says: master the art of the tactical withdrawal.

A tactical withdrawal is a move, chosen deliberately, that preserves your resources for a better engagement. It is very different from running away. Running away is driven by discomfort. A tactical withdrawal is driven by a decision rule you made before the pressure started. You leave on purpose, with your reasons clear and your relationships intact.

## Slide 6 | scene

**On screen:** SORT WHAT YOU HEARD.
**Visual:** scene: a woman on a train with a notebook, writing three headings on a page after a meeting, the city sliding past outside

**Say:**

Before you decide whether to stay or go, you need to know what actually happened in the room. After any hard conversation, sort what you heard into three piles.

## Slide 7 | list

**On screen:** REJECTION. CRITICISM. UNCERTAINTY.

**Say:**

Rejection. A no. It tells you something about fit, timing or budget. It does not tell you about your worth, and it does not need to be argued with. Thank them, and take note.

Criticism. Something specific you can act on. Your price is unclear. The sample took too long to arrive. The pitch went on too long. This is the most valuable pile, even when it stings, because it tells you exactly what to improve.

Uncertainty. They have not decided. Maybe they need to check with someone. Maybe they are not sure. That needs a proportionate follow-up: one clear message at a sensible time. It does not need a flood of chasing emails.

Most people lump all three together into one feeling, usually "it went badly". Sorting them turns a feeling into a plan.

## Slide 8 | scene

**On screen:** THIRD ROUND. WORSE TERMS.
**Visual:** scene (Claire at a long table in a supplier's showroom, sample jars lined up in front of her, a sales manager sliding a revised contract across)

**Say:**

Here is Claire. Her Doom Project is a small natural skincare range, three products to start. She needs a manufacturer to produce the first batch.

She has been negotiating with one supplier for a fortnight. Each round, the terms get a little worse. First a longer wait for payment. Then a higher minimum order. Now a shorter guarantee on the batch.

In the third round she realises the deal no longer meets her limits. The minimum order would take almost all her savings, and the shorter guarantee means she carries the risk if the batch is faulty.

## Slide 9 | compare

**On screen:** Three ways to end the round

**Say:**

She has three ways to end it.

Cave. She accepts, because she has already put in two weeks and she is tired. Her project now carries costs it cannot sustain, and the first faulty batch could end it.

Win. She turns it into a contest. She tells the sales manager this is a disgrace and that she will be telling people. It feels good for about ten seconds. She burns a bridge in a small industry for no gain at all.

Leave, with an alternative. These terms don't work for me. If you can come back to the original minimum and guarantee, I'm ready to sign this week. Otherwise, thank you for your time.

That is a tactical withdrawal. Calm, direct, and governed by a limit she set before the meeting.

## Slide 10 | scene

**On screen:** SHE STANDS UP. CALMLY.
**Visual:** scene: a woman standing and buttoning her coat in a showroom, offering a handshake, the sales manager still seated and surprised

**Say:**

She shakes hands and leaves.

Two days later the supplier calls back and offers the original minimum. She signs. And if they had not called, she had already shortlisted two other manufacturers. Her decision rule meant she was never trapped in one negotiation.

## Slide 11 | list

**On screen:** THE LIMIT. THE DOOR. THE THANKS.

**Say:**

Look at the three parts of her exit, because a clean exit always has them.

The limit. Name plainly what does not work for you. No drama, no accusation. These terms don't work for me.

The door. Say what would make you come back, if anything would. If you can meet the original minimum, I'm ready to sign. Sometimes there is no door, and that is fine too.

The thanks. Leave with the relationship intact. Industries are small, people move jobs, and everyone remembers how you left.

## Slide 12 | scene

**On screen:** THE SAME ARGUMENT. EVERY MONTH.
**Visual:** scene: a small committee room in a sports club, folding chairs, a trophy cabinet, two people leaning across the table at each other while others look at their phones

**Say:**

A second case, with no money in it.

Rhys sits on the committee of his local rugby club. Every monthly meeting, the same two people reopen the same argument about the clubhouse bar prices. It was voted on in the spring. It takes an hour every time, and the decisions that actually need making get rushed at the end.

## Slide 13 | story

**On screen:** He stepped out twice. The item stayed closed.

**Say:**

For months Rhys argued back, which only made the hour longer. So he set a rule for himself.

If an item that has already been decided is reopened without any new facts, I will say so once, and then step out for that part of the meeting.

He told the chair in advance. At the next meeting, the argument started. He said, calmly, this was decided in April, and unless there's something new I'll grab a coffee and come back for the next item. He stepped out. He did it again the following month.

By the third meeting, the chair closed the item before it started. Withdrawal changed the room more than winning the argument ever could have.

## Slide 14 | scene

**On screen:** DECIDE YOUR EXITS IN ADVANCE.
**Visual:** scene: a man writing three short lines on an index card in a quiet kitchen before leaving for a meeting

**Say:**

The point of both stories is the same. Claire and Rhys did not decide to withdraw in the heat of the moment. They had a rule before they walked in.

## Slide 15 | red

**On screen:** CONTINUE. CHANGE. WITHDRAW.

**Say:**

So write your decision rule before you enter the room. It has three parts.

Continue, if the terms stay within your limits and progress is real.

Change, if the goal is still sound but the approach is failing. A different person to talk to, a different offer, a different timescale.

Withdraw, if a limit is crossed, or if the cost of continuing now outweighs the prize.

Bring this week's tools to it. Incentives: what is the other side actually rewarded for, and can they ever give you what you need? Second-order consequences: if you stay, what does that set in motion?

Under pressure, you will not invent a good rule. You will only follow one you already have.

## Slide 16 | scene

**On screen:** EXITS THAT COST YOU.
**Visual:** scene: a door slamming in a corridor, a blur of a figure walking away, papers lifting off a desk in the draught

**Say:**

Here is where people go wrong.

## Slide 17 | list

**On screen:** LEAVING HOT. GHOSTING. QUITTING EARLY.

**Say:**

Leaving hot. Walking out angry. The last line you say is the one they remember, and it is very rarely your best one.

Ghosting. Disappearing instead of closing the conversation properly. It feels easier, and it leaves people confused and your reputation worse.

Quitting early. Calling it a tactical withdrawal when it is really just discomfort. This is the big one. Some conversations are supposed to be uncomfortable. A hard negotiation, honest criticism, a difficult client. Discomfort alone is no reason to leave. Check your decision rule, never your mood.

And never leaving. Some people learn to persist and then cannot stop, staying in deals, jobs and arguments long after the rule said go. Persistence without a rule becomes stubbornness.

## Slide 18 | scene

**On screen:** LEAVE ON PURPOSE.
**Visual:** scene: a man walking calmly down the steps of an office building into evening light, coat on, hands in pockets

**Say:**

Three things to keep.

Sort what you heard into rejection, criticism and uncertainty. Set your exits before you enter the room. And when you leave, leave on purpose: the limit, the door and the thanks.

## Slide 19 | steps

**On screen:** DEBRIEF THE ROOM.

**Say:**

Pause the video when I tell you to.

One. Recall the conversation you held this week, or the most recent hard conversation you have had. Write down what was actually said, factually, without interpretation.

Two. Sort what you heard into three piles: rejection, criticism and uncertainty.

Three. Apply your decision rule. Continue, change or withdraw? Write one sentence explaining why.

Four. Write one proportionate follow-up action, with a date. One message. One revision. One thank-you and goodbye.

Pause now and give it ten minutes.
