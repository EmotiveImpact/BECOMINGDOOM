## Slide 1 | title

**On screen:** HOLD THE LINE.
**Visual:** hood

**Say:**

Last lesson you learned to ask what the other person actually wants. Today you learn to look past the answer you are about to give them, to what follows from it.

Because here is something every experienced negotiator knows. Most bad deals feel good on the day they are made.

## Slide 2 | scene

**On screen:** THEY SAID YES. EVERYONE SHOOK HANDS.
**Visual:** scene: two people shaking hands across a desk in a bright office, a signed contract between them, both relieved

**Say:**

The immediate result is easy to see. They said yes. The sale closed. The argument ended. The awkward silence went away. Everyone shook hands.

It feels like a win, and sometimes it is.

## Slide 3 | question

**On screen:** And then what?

**Say:**

The question that protects you is: and then what?

What happens next month, when they expect the same again? What happens when someone else hears about it? What happens to you, six months from now, living with the thing you agreed to today?

## Slide 4 | scene

**On screen:** THE EASY YES HAS A LONG TAIL.
**Visual:** scene: a man at his desk months later, an email from the same client open on screen asking for "the usual rate", his hand over his mouth

**Say:**

Most of the decisions that hurt us were easy at the time. The yes that ended an uncomfortable conversation. The discount that closed the sale. The favour that became an expectation.

The cost arrived later, quietly, after we had forgotten making the decision.

## Slide 5 | red

**On screen:** WHAT FOLLOWS THE IMMEDIATE RESULT?

**Say:**

The sixth thinking tool is second-order consequences. The question: what follows the immediate result?

First-order thinking asks whether this works today. Second-order thinking asks what this sets in motion.

Most people stop at the first order, because the first order is where the relief is. The people who build things that last learn to keep looking.

## Slide 6 | scene

**On screen:** ASK "AND THEN WHAT?" TWICE.
**Visual:** scene: a single stone dropped into a still black pond, rings spreading outwards under a red light

**Say:**

The method is almost embarrassingly simple.

## Slide 7 | list

**On screen:** FIRST ORDER. SECOND ORDER. THIRD ORDER.

**Say:**

Ask "and then what?", and then ask it again.

First order. The result today. They say yes, and you get the deal.

Second order. What that result changes. They now expect the same terms next time.

Third order. What that change sets up. Other clients hear about it and ask for the same. Your prices quietly drop across the board.

You rarely need to go further than three steps. Beyond that you are mostly guessing.

## Slide 8 | scene

**On screen:** ONE YES. MANY RIPPLES.
**Visual:** scene: a small video studio, cameras on stands and a lighting rig, two men reviewing footage on a monitor

**Say:**

And that brings us to the limit on this tool. Speculation is not prediction.

Every ripple you trace is a guess about the future. Some will be good guesses. Some will be wrong. So say how confident you are in each one. "Quite likely" and "possible but unlikely" belong in the same sheet, clearly labelled. Then decide.

## Slide 9 | story

**On screen:** Thirty per cent off wins the sale.

**Say:**

Here is Diego. He runs a small video production studio with one other person. His Doom Project is landing his first corporate client, because corporate work pays three times what weddings and local adverts pay.

He is close. A regional housebuilder wants a series of six videos. In the final meeting their marketing manager asks for thirty per cent off.

Diego wants this badly. It would be the biggest contract he has ever signed. The first-order answer is obvious: say yes and win.

He takes a breath and asks what follows.

## Slide 10 | scene

**On screen:** IT ALSO SETS NEXT YEAR'S PRICE.
**Visual:** scene (Diego in a corporate meeting room, a printed quote on the table, the marketing manager across from him, Diego writing something small in the margin)

**Say:**

Second order: the discounted price becomes his price for this client. Every future project with them starts thirty per cent down.

Third order: corporate clients talk, and the marketing manager will move jobs one day. His first corporate rate becomes his reputation. And at that price, he cannot afford the second crew member he needs to deliver six videos properly. The quality drops on the most important work he has ever done.

## Slide 11 | compare

**On screen:** Three answers to the same ask

**Say:**

So he has three answers available.

Yes. He wins the sale and anchors his price low for years. He also overworks himself and his colleague to deliver it, and the videos are weaker than they should be.

Never. He protects his price, says it is non-negotiable, and offers nothing else. The client walks, and he has protected a price on a contract he no longer has.

Trade. He holds the price and offers alternatives. I can't move the rate for this scope. What I can do is four videos instead of six, which fits your budget. Or we start with one paid pilot video at full rate, and if it performs, we agree the rest.

They choose the pilot. It performs. They sign for the other five at full price.

## Slide 12 | scene

**On screen:** MOVE ONLY IN EXCHANGE FOR SOMETHING.
**Visual:** scene: two hands sliding a single sheet of paper across a table, a pen resting on top

**Say:**

Holding the line still leaves room to move. You simply move something only in exchange for something. Less scope for less money. A pilot for a commitment. A faster payment for a small discount.

And you prepare all of it before you walk in. A clear request. Two alternatives. And a limit: the point below which you will politely leave. Decided while you are calm, because you will not make a good decision with someone waiting for your answer.

## Slide 13 | red

**On screen:** KILLER INSTINCT. NO PERMISSION NEEDED.

**Say:**

Two principles meet here.

Principle three, develop your killer instinct, means making a clear request and being prepared to hear no. Diego asked for full price on a pilot. That takes nerve.

And principle eight, burn the bridge of permission, comes back from week one. Diego did not need the client's permission to value his own work. He set his price, and he let them decide. Their decision was theirs. His price was his.

## Slide 14 | scene

**On screen:** SHE RENTS OUT ONE FLAT.
**Visual:** scene: a woman in her fifties at a kitchen table, a laptop open on a banking page, a phone showing a message from a tenant

**Say:**

A second case, much smaller, and much more common.

Ella lets out one flat, the one she lived in before she moved in with her partner. Her tenant messages to ask if she can pay late this month. She has lost some shifts.

## Slide 15 | story

**On screen:** Yes, by the fifteenth, in writing.

**Say:**

First order: say yes. It is easy and kind, the tenant is relieved, and the conversation ends.

Second order: with no date agreed, late can quietly become normal. Not out of bad faith, simply because there is no line. And Ella's own mortgage payment on the flat leaves her account on the first of every month.

Third order: if it slips for three months, Ella either has a crisis or a confrontation, and the relationship she wanted to protect is the first casualty.

So she says yes, by the fifteenth, and confirms it in a short message. Of course, no problem. Can we say the fifteenth? Let me know if anything changes before then.

It is still kind. It protects both of them. The tenant pays on the fourteenth.

## Slide 16 | scene

**On screen:** CHEAP TODAY. COSTLY LATER.
**Visual:** scene: a till receipt for a small amount pinned beside a much longer invoice on a corkboard

**Say:**

Some of the most expensive decisions you will make feel cheap on the day. Price the future cost before you agree.

## Slide 17 | list

**On screen:** WHERE FORESIGHT FAILS.

**Say:**

Here is where people go wrong.

Paralysis. They chase consequences for ever and never decide. Three steps is enough. Then choose.

Only downside. They forget that second-order effects can be good. A fair price now can win referrals later. A generous gesture can build a reputation. Trace the good ripples as well.

No limit set. They walk in without a line they have already decided not to cross, so the line gets drawn under pressure, in the worst possible place.

And the fortune teller. They treat their guesses about the future as certainties, then argue with people about things that have not happened. Label your confidence.

## Slide 18 | scene

**On screen:** ASK "AND THEN WHAT?"
**Visual:** scene: a man walking out of an office building at dusk, phone to his ear, smiling, a folder under his arm

**Say:**

Three things to keep.

Ask "and then what?", twice. Price the future cost, and label how confident you are. And hold the line by offering options instead of a flat no.

## Slide 19 | steps

**On screen:** PREPARE YOUR LINE.

**Say:**

Pause the video when I tell you to.

One. Write the clear request you will make in your next important conversation. One sentence.

Two. Imagine three responses: they accept, they negotiate, or they decline. For each one, write what it leads to two steps on. Include at least one future cost.

Three. Prepare two alternatives you could offer if they push back. Each one should trade something for something.

Four. Set the limit you will not cross, and write it down now, while you are calm.

Pause now and give it ten minutes.
