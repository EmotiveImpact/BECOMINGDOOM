## Slide 1 | title

**On screen:** EVERY YES SPENDS YOUR LIFE.
**Visual:** hood

**Say:**

Welcome to week two: build the fortress.

Last week you claimed your own life as worth investing in. You wrote a doctrine, you said a first no and you put a label on trial. This week asks a harder question. Can you trust yourself to execute, week after week, when life pushes back?

It starts with one uncomfortable truth. Every yes spends your life.

## Slide 2 | scene

**On screen:** IT ONLY TOOK TWO HOURS.
**Visual:** scene: a man helping a colleague at her desk late in the office, the windows dark, his coat over the back of a chair

**Say:**

We describe favours by their size. It's only two hours. It's just a quick call. It won't take long.

Size is the wrong question.

## Slide 3 | question

**On screen:** Two hours of what?

**Say:**

The right question is what those two hours would otherwise have been.

Two hours of your project. Two hours of sleep. Two hours with your children. Two hours of the walk that keeps your head straight.

Time does not arrive empty. Every hour you have is already holding something, even if that something is rest. The price of a yes is whatever it pushes out.

## Slide 4 | scene

**On screen:** THERE IS NO THIRD POT OF TIME.
**Visual:** scene: a kitchen wall calendar crowded with handwriting, one evening crossed out and rewritten twice

**Say:**

Most people run their week as if there were a hidden reserve somewhere. A spare evening that will appear if they just say yes now and sort it out later.

There is no hidden reserve. When you add something, something else leaves. The only choice you get is whether you pick what leaves, or let it be picked for you.

## Slide 5 | red

**On screen:** WHAT IS THE BEST REALISTIC ALTERNATIVE I GIVE UP?

**Say:**

This is the first of the ten thinking tools in this course. Opportunity cost.

The question is: what is the best realistic alternative I give up?

Two words carry the weight. Best, because you compare against the most valuable thing you could have done, never the least. Realistic, because you compare against what you would actually have done, never a fantasy version of you who works perfectly every evening.

## Slide 6 | scene

**On screen:** THE REAL PRICE INCLUDES THE TRAVEL.
**Visual:** scene: a man on a late train, tie loosened, laptop bag on his lap, staring out at dark fields

**Say:**

Opportunity cost is an old idea from economics. You do not need the textbook. You need the habit of asking the question before you agree to something, instead of discovering the answer on Sunday night when the week has gone.

## Slide 7 | list

**On screen:** THREE QUESTIONS PER YES.

**Say:**

Pricing a yes takes three questions.

First. What, exactly, am I agreeing to? Include the preparation, the travel, the waiting and the follow-up. A two-hour favour is often four hours once you count the edges.

Second. Where does it come from? Name the actual hours, the energy or the money. Thursday evening. Saturday's run. Forty pounds from the project budget.

Third. Is it worth more than the best thing it replaces? Sometimes the answer is yes. That is fine. Saying yes is allowed. What changes is that now it is a choice you made, with the price in view.

## Slide 8 | scene

**On screen:** SIX HOURS A WEEK. TWO GONE IN ONE MESSAGE.
**Visual:** scene: a phone on a desk showing a message, "Could you help me with the deck tonight? Two hours max", beside an open project notebook

**Say:**

Put it in numbers. This course asks for about six hours a week. One two-hour favour takes a third of it.

That does not make the favour wrong. It makes the price visible. Once you can see the price, you can decide whether you want to pay it.

## Slide 9 | story

**On screen:** Thursday. Two hours. Tonight's session or tonight's sleep.

**Say:**

Here is Ravi. He is a project accountant, he has two young children, and his Doom Project gets one protected evening a week: Thursday, eight until ten, after the children are asleep.

On Thursday afternoon a colleague asks for two hours of help with a presentation she is giving on Friday. She is stressed. Ravi likes her, and he knows the material.

He is also already tired. If he says yes, the two hours come from one of two places. His project session, or his sleep. There is no third pot.

## Slide 10 | scene

**On screen:** PAY WITH SLEEP. PAY WITH THE PROJECT. OR CHOOSE.
**Visual:** scene: Ravi at his kitchen table at night, a child's drawing on the fridge behind him, laptop open, rubbing his eyes

**Say:**

He has three ways to pay.

## Slide 11 | compare

**On screen:** Three ways to pay for one yes

**Say:**

Pay with sleep. He helps for two hours and then does his project session anyway, finishing at one in the morning. It feels heroic. It costs him Friday, a short temper with his children on Saturday, and a weekend spent recovering.

Pay with the project. He helps and the project slips another week. Once is nothing. If he does this every week, the project never happens, and in six weeks he will tell himself he did not have time.

Name the cost first, then choose. He offers one hour at four o'clock, before he leaves the office, and keeps the evening. Or he decides this colleague and this presentation matter enough to give the full two hours, and moves his session to Sunday morning. Deliberately, out loud, in the calendar.

Either version of that third option is fine. Two of them involve saying yes. What matters is that he chose which cost to bear, instead of letting the cost choose him.

## Slide 13 | red

**On screen:** RADICAL ACCOUNTABILITY IS ABSOLUTE POWER.

**Say:**

Principle nine says: radical accountability is absolute power.

It means you own your controllable decisions, including the price of them. When Ravi chose to give the colleague two hours and move his session, he owned that. When the session then slipped on Sunday because his son was ill, that was not his fault, and accountability does not mean pretending it was.

Accountability is power because it puts the decisions back in your hands. If every lost evening is somebody else's fault, then nothing you do will fix it. If you can point at the decisions that were yours, you can make different ones next week.

## Slide 14 | scene

**On screen:** FORTY LOAVES A DAY.
**Visual:** scene: a woman in a flour-dusted apron at the counter of a small bakery before dawn, ovens glowing behind her, a café owner holding a clipboard

**Say:**

A second case, with money in it, because opportunity cost is not only about time.

Grace runs a small bakery with one part-time assistant. A busy café asks for forty loaves a day, six days a week, at a discounted wholesale price. It sounds like growth. It sounds like the kind of thing you are supposed to say yes to.

## Slide 15 | story

**On screen:** The opportunity was real. So was its cost.

**Say:**

Before she answers, Grace writes the ledger.

Forty loaves a day means a four o'clock start, six days a week. It means her assistant's hours go on the wholesale order. And it means the new pastry range she has been planning for a year, the thing she thinks will actually grow her shop, never gets developed. The discount means the margin is thin, so the money barely covers the extra flour and labour.

So she makes a counter-offer. Twenty loaves, three days a week, at a fair price. The café takes it. She keeps two mornings for the new range, and the range launches in the spring.

The opportunity was real. So was its cost. She saw both before she agreed, which is the whole point of the tool.

## Slide 16 | scene

**On screen:** REST IS A LEGITIMATE USE OF TIME.
**Visual:** scene: a man asleep on a sofa on a Sunday afternoon, a book open on his chest, sunlight across the floor

**Say:**

One limit on this tool, and it matters.

Do not price every free hour as if it could earn unlimited money. People who learn opportunity cost sometimes start treating rest as a waste and every evening off as a failure. That is a misuse of the tool.

Rest is a legitimate use of time. Seeing friends is a legitimate use of time. Opportunity cost is about honest comparison. It is never a reason to feel guilty for resting.

## Slide 17 | list

**On screen:** HOW THE LEDGER LIES.

**Say:**

Here is where people go wrong when they use it.

The worst-case comparison. You compare the yes against scrolling on your phone, so everything looks worth doing. Compare it against your best realistic alternative.

Hidden hours. You forget the preparation, the travel and the recovery afterwards, so every commitment looks cheaper than it is.

Guilt maths. You quietly add points for how bad saying no would feel. Feelings matter, and they are worth noticing. They should not set the price.

And the frozen ledger. You price one yes and then never look again, so a commitment that made sense in January is still eating your Thursdays in June. Prices change. Check them.

## Slide 18 | scene

**On screen:** EVERY YES IS PAID FOR SOMEWHERE.
**Visual:** scene: a till receipt curling on a café table beside a cold coffee and a diary

**Say:**

Three things to keep.

Every yes is paid for somewhere. Compare it against the best realistic alternative, never the worst. And once it is priced, a yes becomes a choice you can stand behind.

## Slide 19 | steps

**On screen:** BUILD YOUR LEDGER.

**Say:**

Pause the video when I tell you to.

One. List three commitments you made in the last week. Big or small. A meeting, a favour, a social plan, a subscription.

Two. Beside each one, write what it actually replaced. Be specific. Not "time": write Tuesday evening, or my morning run, or forty pounds.

Three. Mark the one whose price you would not pay again.

Four. Draft one sentence to renegotiate it. Shorter, later, less often, or not at all.

Pause now and give it five minutes.
