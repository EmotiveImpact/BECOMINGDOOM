## Slide 1 | title

**On screen:** BODY, STANDARDS AND RECOVERY.
**Visual:** hood

**Say:**

Every session you run, every conversation you hold and every decision you make runs on one piece of equipment. Your body.

You have priced your time and you have built the room. This lesson is about keeping the equipment ready, and about what you do on the day it lets you down. Because that day is coming.

## Slide 2 | scene

**On screen:** YOU MISSED A SESSION.
**Visual:** scene: an empty desk chair in the morning light, a laptop closed, a calendar notification on a phone reading "Project session: missed"

**Say:**

At some point in the next five weeks you will miss a session. You will be ill. A train will be cancelled. Somebody you love will need you. Or you will simply not show up, and you will not be entirely sure why.

## Slide 3 | question

**On screen:** Now what do you do?

**Say:**

What you do in the next twenty-four hours matters far more than the miss itself.

Most programmes do not die on the hard days. They die on the day after a missed one, when somebody decides that the streak is broken, the week is ruined, and they might as well start again on Monday. Then Monday becomes next month.

So today you are going to build two things. A floor for your body that you can hold even in a bad week, and a reset you can run the moment something breaks.

## Slide 4 | scene

**On screen:** THE MACHINE EVERYTHING RUNS ON.
**Visual:** scene: a woman tying her trainers on a doorstep at dawn, breath visible in the cold air

**Say:**

Start with the body.

## Slide 5 | red

**On screen:** TREAT YOUR BODY AS THE ULTIMATE WEAPON.

**Say:**

Principle twelve says: treat your body as the ultimate weapon.

Think about how a serious person treats a serious tool. It is maintained. You clean it, you store it properly, you know its limits and you do not abuse it to prove a point.

Sleep, movement, food and recovery are the maintenance. None of it is a reward you earn after the work is done. It is what makes the work possible in the first place. A tired mind makes worse decisions, holds worse conversations and quits sooner. Look after the equipment.

## Slide 6 | scene

**On screen:** SET THE FLOOR. FORGET THE FANTASY.
**Visual:** scene: a man doing press-ups on a living-room rug while a kettle boils in the background

**Say:**

The mistake most people make is to set a fantasy instead of a floor. A fantasy is the plan you would follow if every day were perfect. Five o'clock starts, an hour in the gym, no sugar, eight hours of sleep.

A floor is the minimum you will hold on your worst week. It is much smaller, and it is much more powerful, because you will actually keep it.

## Slide 7 | list

**On screen:** THE FOUR FLOORS.

**Say:**

Think of four floors.

Sleep. A fixed boundary, protected like a meeting. Choose the time your screens go off and stick to it. A late-night catch-up borrows from tomorrow at a terrible rate of interest.

Movement. A daily minimum small enough to do on your worst day. A ten-minute walk. Some stretching. Twenty squats while the kettle boils. Something your body can do, adapted to any health constraints you have.

Fuel. Real meals before long sessions, with coffee alongside them, never instead of them.

Recovery. One genuinely off evening each week, planned in advance and protected like a work session. Rest that is chosen recharges you. Rest that is stolen just leaves guilt behind.

Fitness goals are a separate matter, and you can have them too if they matter to you. The floors exist to keep the machine running for six weeks and beyond.

## Slide 8 | scene

**On screen:** THIS IS NOT MEDICAL ADVICE.
**Visual:** scene: a GP's waiting room, a man reading a leaflet, a woman with a walking stick beside him

**Say:**

One thing to say clearly. Bodies differ. Some of you live with a health condition, an injury, a disability or a pregnancy. Some of you work physical jobs that already leave you exhausted.

Your floors are yours to set, for your body and your circumstances. If you have a health condition, follow the advice of a qualified professional. Nothing in this course is medical advice, and nothing in it asks you to push through pain or skip sleep to prove you are serious.

## Slide 9 | story

**On screen:** She missed Sunday. Monday decides the week.

**Say:**

Now for the miss.

Lucia is a physiotherapy assistant. She is fit, she is organised and she takes the course seriously. Her Doom Project is the first module of a short online course for people recovering from knee surgery.

She planned a long project session on Sunday afternoon. Family plans ran long. Lunch became dinner, dinner became a late drive home, and she missed the session entirely.

On Monday morning she has a choice about what kind of week this becomes.

## Slide 10 | scene

**On screen:** THE SPIRAL. THE PUNISHMENT. THE RESET.
**Visual:** scene: Lucia at her kitchen counter early on Monday, coffee in one hand, notebook open, looking at a crossed-out Sunday

**Say:**

She has three ways to respond.

## Slide 11 | compare

**On screen:** Three responses to the same miss

**Say:**

The spiral. The week is ruined, so she will start again properly next Monday. She feels a little relieved, because the pressure is off. Most programmes die exactly here.

The punishment. She stays up until two on Monday night to catch up on everything she missed. It feels like accountability. It feels like being serious. On Tuesday she is exhausted, snaps at a patient's relative, and loses that day too. Punishment looks like discipline and behaves like the spiral.

The reset. Notice, record, repair, resume. She notices the miss without drama. She writes one line in her log: Sunday lost to family, my choice to stay, fine. She repairs with a forty-minute session on Monday evening, on the first task only, at her normal time. And she resumes the plan as written on Tuesday.

## Slide 12 | scene

**On screen:** NOTICE. RECORD. REPAIR. RESUME.
**Visual:** scene: four short lines written in a notebook, the word "Resume" underlined twice in red

**Say:**

That is the reset. Four steps.

Notice: something broke. Name it plainly, without a speech about your character.

Record: one line. What happened, and which part was in your control. This is where last lesson's tool helps. Was it decided, influenced or something you could only respond to?

Repair: the smallest useful session you can run in the next twenty-four hours. Small on purpose, because a giant repair is how punishment sneaks back in.

Resume: go back to the plan as written. No new plan, no fresh start, no Monday.

## Slide 13 | red

**On screen:** OWN THE MISS. OWN THE REPAIR.

**Say:**

That is principle nine, radical accountability, applied to yourself. You own the miss and you own the repair.

Notice what accountability leaves out. It leaves out self-punishment. It leaves out blaming yourself for things that genuinely were not yours, like illness, or a family emergency, or a cancelled train. Owning your controllable decisions means being honest about which ones were controllable. Sometimes the honest answer is: nothing, and that is fine. You still run the repair.

## Slide 14 | scene

**On screen:** THREE HOURS' SLEEP.
**Visual:** scene: a man in a dark hallway at night holding a crying baby against his shoulder, a toddler's toys on the floor

**Say:**

A second case.

Andre is an electrician with two children under four. His Doom Project is pricing and launching a small home-safety inspection service. His protected session is half past five in the morning, before the house wakes up.

Last night the baby was up for hours. Andre slept for about three. At half past five his alarm goes, and the session is not happening.

## Slide 15 | story

**On screen:** The smallest possible session. The promise survives.

**Say:**

The old Andre would have done one of two things. Dragged himself to the table and stared at a screen for an hour, getting nothing done and feeling worse. Or written the whole week off.

This time he runs the reset. Notice: missed, baby awake all night. Record: one line in his log, outside my control. Repair: twenty minutes at lunch in the van, on the easiest task, which is writing three questions for a customer survey. Resume: tomorrow at half past five as planned.

It is the smallest possible session. What survives is the promise to himself, and over six weeks that matters far more than any single morning's minutes.

## Slide 16 | scene

**On screen:** NO ZERO DAYS. NO MARTYRS.
**Visual:** scene: a man eating a proper breakfast at a kitchen table, plate of eggs and toast, notebook beside him

**Say:**

Keep two rules in tension.

No zero days. Something small always counts. Twenty minutes, three questions, one paragraph.

And no martyrs. Nobody earns anything in this course by wrecking their sleep or their health. Suffering is not proof that you care.

## Slide 17 | list

**On screen:** STANDARDS THAT BREAK YOU.

**Say:**

Here is where people go wrong.

All or nothing. A plan that only works on perfect days fails on the first ordinary one.

Borrowed sleep. Late nights paid for with tomorrow's focus, at a terrible rate of interest.

Someone else's plan. A routine copied from an athlete, an influencer or a colleague with a different body, job and family. Choose yours. Build it around your life.

And the shrinking floor. Some people set a sensible floor and then let it slide a little each week, until the minimum becomes nothing. A floor is a floor. If it genuinely needs to change, change it deliberately and write down why.

## Slide 18 | scene

**On screen:** A MISS IS INFORMATION.
**Visual:** scene: a bedside table at night: phone face down and charging, a book, a glass of water, the lamp about to be switched off

**Say:**

Three things to keep.

Maintain your body; never punish it. A miss is information, so read it and keep going. And when something breaks, run the reset: notice, record, repair, resume.

## Slide 19 | steps

**On screen:** WRITE YOUR FLOORS AND YOUR RESET.

**Say:**

Pause the video when I tell you to.

One. Your movement floor for a bad week. Something you could do on your worst day.

Two. Your sleep boundary, with an actual time. Screens off at.

Three. The shortest session that still counts as keeping the promise. Fifteen minutes? Twenty? Choose a number.
