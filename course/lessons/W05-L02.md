## Slide 1 | title

**On screen:** CLOSE THE LOOP.
**Visual:** hood

**Say:**

Yesterday you wrote a leverage hypothesis. Today it costs this much, I will change this, I expect that.

Now you need a way to find out whether it is true. This lesson is about building a feedback loop: a signal you can watch, a time to look at it, and a rule for what you will change when you do.

## Slide 2 | scene

**On screen:** "I LOVE IT."
**Visual:** scene: a man showing an app on his phone to a friend in a pub, the friend grinning and giving a thumbs up

**Say:**

You show someone your project and they say they love it. It feels wonderful. You go home lighter. You tell your partner it went really well.

## Slide 3 | question

**On screen:** Did they use it?

**Say:**

Then ask the harder question. Did they use it?

Did they finish the task it was built for? Did they come back the next day? Did they pay, book, sign up or tell a friend without being asked?

Praise is about how people feel in front of you. Behaviour is about what they do when you are not there. Only one of those will tell you whether your project works.

## Slide 4 | scene

**On screen:** EVERYTHING THAT IMPROVES HAS A LOOP.
**Visual:** scene: a cyclist glancing at a small screen on his handlebars on a long road, the numbers lit in red

**Say:**

Everything that gets better over time has a loop. A cyclist watches his power output and adjusts his effort. A chef tastes the sauce and adds salt. A thermostat reads the temperature and switches the heating on.

Signal, check, adjust. Over and over.

## Slide 5 | red

**On screen:** WHICH SIGNAL LEADS TO WHICH ADJUSTMENT, AND WHEN?

**Say:**

The eighth thinking tool is feedback loops. The question: which signal leads to which adjustment, and when?

Three parts. A signal you can observe. A review interval, so you actually look at it. And an adjustment rule, so that looking changes something.

Most people have the first part and nothing else. They collect information and never decide what it means.

## Slide 6 | scene

**On screen:** WATCH WHAT THEY DO.
**Visual:** scene: a man standing quietly behind someone using a laptop at a kitchen table, watching their hands, a notebook in his own

**Say:**

The hardest part is choosing the signal.

## Slide 7 | list

**On screen:** A USEFUL SIGNAL.

**Say:**

A useful signal has three qualities.

Behaviour. It records what people do. Completed the set-up. Came back on day three. Booked a second session. Things people do are far harder to fake than things people say, including to themselves.

Tied to the result. It moves when the real outcome moves. If your project exists to help people build a habit, the signal is whether they keep the habit, not how many times they opened the app.

Cheap to read. You can check it in minutes, every week. If it takes a day to measure, you will stop measuring by the second week.

## Slide 8 | scene

**On screen:** SIGNAL. FRIDAY. RULE.
**Visual:** scene: a wall calendar with every Friday circled in red and the word REVIEW written beside each one

**Say:**

Here is what a complete loop looks like for a simple app.

The signal: how many of ten testers completed the set-up. The interval: review every Friday at five. The rule: if fewer than five complete it, revise the step where most people get stuck, and change only that step.

Every number there is one you would collect or set yourself. None of them is a prediction about your project.

## Slide 9 | story

**On screen:** Ten glowing messages.

**Say:**

Here is Sam. He works in a call centre and taught himself to code in the evenings. His Doom Project is a small habit-tracking app that does one thing: it asks you each evening whether you did the one habit you chose, and shows you a simple streak.

He gives it to ten testers, mostly friends and colleagues. The messages that come back are glowing. Love this. So clean. Brilliant idea. Everyone says it is great.

## Slide 10 | scene

**On screen:** TWO OF THEM GOT STUCK ON THE SAME SCREEN.
**Visual:** scene (Sam sitting slightly behind a tester on a sofa, both looking at a phone, the tester frowning at a settings screen)

**Say:**

Then he does something most people never do. He asks two of the testers if he can watch them set it up, over their shoulder, without saying anything.

Both get stuck at the same screen: the one where you choose a reminder time. The design makes it look as if you have to create an account first. Both of them nearly give up.

When he checks the numbers, only three of the ten testers have actually finished setting it up. The other seven sent lovely messages about an app they had never really used.

## Slide 11 | compare

**On screen:** Three ways to read the same week

**Say:**

He has three ways to read it.

Count the compliments. Ten kind messages. The app stays exactly as it is, and stays mostly unused. That is the limit on this tool: avoid optimising a vanity measure instead of the intended result. Likes, compliments and downloads feel like progress. They are often vanity.

Track everything. He installs analytics and measures twenty things. Screen time, taps, session length, retention by day. He has no rule for any of it, so he spends his evenings staring at dashboards instead of fixing anything.

One signal, one rule. Set-up completion is the signal. Friday is the review. Under half means he fixes the screen where people stall. He redesigns that one screen, sends the update, and checks again the following Friday. Seven out of ten complete it. Now he moves to the next signal: how many are still using it after a week.

## Slide 12 | scene

**On screen:** OWN THE RESULT. NOT THE PRAISE.
**Visual:** scene: a man at a desk late at night redesigning a single phone screen on a large monitor, a sketch of it on paper beside the keyboard

**Say:**

Principle nine, radical accountability, comes back here.

You own the result your users actually get, not just the praise they give you. Praise is pleasant, and you can keep it. But Sam's job was never to be complimented. It was to help people keep a habit. Accountability means looking at the number that tells you whether you did.

## Slide 13 | red

**On screen:** CHAOS INTO CURRENCY.

**Say:**

And principle ten, manipulate chaos into currency, is at work too.

Every confusing result, every drop-off, every complaint is a little piece of chaos. Most people find it painful and look away. A good feedback loop turns that chaos into currency: specific information about exactly what to fix next. The worse the news, the more useful it often is.

## Slide 14 | scene

**On screen:** TWELVE. NINE. SIX.
**Visual:** scene: a living room set up for a book club, eight chairs in a circle, only five of them filled, a plate of biscuits barely touched

**Say:**

A second case.

Aisha runs a neighbourhood book club. It started with twelve regulars. Members tell her it is the best part of their month, and they mean it.

The signal says something else. Attendance goes from twelve, to nine, to six, over three months.

## Slide 15 | story

**On screen:** Under eight: ask the people who stopped coming.

**Say:**

Aisha has a rule, set when she started. If attendance drops under eight, she asks the people who stopped coming, rather than the people who still turn up.

So she messages the six who left. Short and friendly: no pressure, I'd just love to know what would make it easier to come.

Five reply. Four say the same thing. The books are too long. They feel embarrassed turning up halfway through. The people still attending were the ones with time to finish five-hundred-page novels.

She switches to shorter books and the occasional long one with two months to read it. Within three months, attendance is back to eleven. The kind words never told her any of that. The empty chairs did.

## Slide 16 | scene

**On screen:** KIND WORDS ARE NOT EVIDENCE.
**Visual:** scene: a card reading "Love it!!" lying beside an unopened product box on a shelf

**Say:**

Kind words are pleasant, and the people who say them usually mean them. They are a poor guide to what is actually happening. Watch what people do.

## Slide 17 | list

**On screen:** LOOPS THAT NEVER CLOSE.

**Say:**

Here is where people go wrong.

No review date. The data is collected and never looked at. A signal you never check is just a number in a drawer.

Changing everything at once. Five changes in one week, so you cannot tell which one worked. Change one thing, then check.

Asking only friends. Kind people are a poor measuring instrument. Watch strangers use it, or at least watch friends without helping them.

And moving the threshold. The result comes in below your rule, so you quietly decide the rule was too strict. Set the threshold before you look, and respect it when you do. You can change a rule, deliberately, at a review. You cannot change it because you did not like the answer.

## Slide 18 | scene

**On screen:** ONE SIGNAL. ONE RULE. ONE DATE.
**Visual:** scene: a phone calendar alert reading "Friday review: set-up completion", a hand reaching for it

**Say:**

Three things to keep.

Watch behaviour, not praise. One signal, one rule, set before you look. And review it on a date that is already in your calendar.

## Slide 19 | steps

**On screen:** BUILD YOUR LOOP.

**Say:**

Pause the video when I tell you to.

One. Name the result your audience should get from your Doom Project. Use your audience statement from yesterday.

Two. Choose one signal that shows it actually happened. Something people do, that you can check in minutes.

Three. Put a review date and time in your calendar. Before Day 33, so you can revise in time.

Four. Write your adjustment rule. If the signal is below this, then I change that one thing.

And add one limit: what you will not do to get the number up. No pressure on people, no misleading them, no paying for fake results.

Pause now and give it five minutes.
