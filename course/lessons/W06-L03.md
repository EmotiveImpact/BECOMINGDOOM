# W06-L03: A finite life; a chosen next chapter

Video 18 of 18 • Week 6: Become Dangerous • Full lesson, version 2

- **Principles:** P14 Embrace your finite nature; P01 Put yourself above all people (closing the loop)
- **Model:** all ten, chosen by relevance
- **Leads to:** workbook page W06-D05 (Day 40), Doomsday (Day 41) and the ninety-day plan (Day 42)
- **Evidence:** A before and after comparison, an operating code and a continuation plan
- **Lead case:** Daniel, the designer from lesson 1, returns at the end of his six weeks
- **Second case:** Sophie, Vietnamese British woman, early 30s, a graduate of an earlier cohort, six months on
- **Hooded figure:** the faceless figure, the face a pitch-black void: the end of the six-week arc

## Slide 1 | title

**On screen:** A FINITE LIFE. A CHOSEN NEXT CHAPTER.
**Visual:** hood (the faceless figure for the first time in full: hood up, the face a pitch-black void)

**Say:**

This is the final lesson of Becoming Doom.

Six weeks ago you were asked whose life you were building. Since then you have written a doctrine, said no, tested a label, priced your yeses, built a room for work, made something real, held your ground with people and put an offer into the world.

Today you decide what comes next. And you decide it the same way you have learned to decide everything else in this course: deliberately, with the cost in view.

## Slide 2 | scene

**On screen:** SIX WEEKS AGO.
**Visual:** scene: the kitchen table from lesson 1 again, late at night, but now the notebook is open, full of writing, and the client work is closed

**Say:**

Think back to the first lesson. The notebook on the edge of the table. The thing you kept saying mattered, that had never been allowed to cost anything.

Look at where it is now.

## Slide 3 | question

**On screen:** What did you build, and what did it cost?

**Say:**

Whatever the answer, it is yours. For some of you the project has gone further than you hoped. For some it went somewhere unexpected. For a few it was withdrawn, deliberately, and something better took its place. All of those are honest results.

The question that matters is whether you chose. Whether the time went where you decided it should, and whether you can show the evidence.

## Slide 4 | scene

**On screen:** THE SAME SCENARIO. SIX WEEKS LATER.
**Visual:** scene: two sheets of paper side by side on a desk, the same questions on each, the handwriting on the right firmer and shorter

**Say:**

Before Day 1, you answered a scenario. Two protected hours, a request for a favour during them, and a project you were unsure about.

Today you answer it again, without looking at your first answer. Then you compare them.

That comparison is some of the most honest evidence you will collect in this course. Nobody else marks it. You simply look at who answered the first time, and who is answering now.

## Slide 5 | red

**On screen:** EMBRACE YOUR FINITE NATURE.

**Say:**

Principle fourteen says: embrace your finite nature.

Your time is limited. That can sound like a gloomy thought. In this course it is the reason any of this matters.

If you had unlimited time, no choice would cost anything. You could do everything, eventually. Because you cannot, every choice you make is a statement of what you value. Every yes spends your life, as you learned in week two. A finite life is exactly what makes your choices mean something.

## Slide 6 | scene

**On screen:** THREE EXCITING DIRECTIONS.
**Visual:** scene (Daniel at his desk, his rebuilt portfolio live on one screen, three sticky notes on the monitor edge reading GROW STUDIO, WRITE BOOK, TEACH COURSE)

**Say:**

Here is Daniel again, the designer from the first lesson. Six weeks ago he could not find one Thursday evening for his portfolio.

His portfolio is now live. He has used it in three pitches and won one new client at a better rate than any he has had before. He has stopped doing unpaid revisions. He has a system.

And now he has three exciting directions. Grow the studio, with a second designer. Write a short book on branding for small businesses. Or teach a weekend course for new freelancers.

## Slide 7 | story

**On screen:** Choosing one path means naming what stays undone.

**Say:**

Here is the hardest part of planning the next ninety days. Choosing one path means naming the attractive alternatives you will leave undone.

If your plan includes everything, it is a wish list. A real plan says what you are not doing, and you make peace with that on purpose.

So Daniel runs the tools. Opportunity cost: what does each direction push out? Probabilistic thinking: which has the strongest evidence so far? He has a paying client and two warm leads for the studio, and no evidence yet for the book or the course.

He chooses one sustainable milestone for the next ninety days. Five clients at his new rate, and a trial week with a second designer. Then he writes one more sentence, and it is the most important line on the page. The book and the course stay undone until the ninety-day review.

That sentence is what makes the plan real.

## Slide 8 | scene

**On screen:** YOUR OPERATING CODE.
**Visual:** scene: a single page pinned to a cork board above a desk, four headed sections in bold capitals, a red pin at each corner

**Say:**

Look at what you have built over six weeks. A doctrine. Boundaries. A command sheet. A reset. A Doombot. Decision rules. A feedback loop. Margins. Ten thinking tools.

Your operating code is a single page that says how you will keep using all of this without anyone prompting you. There is no next lesson coming. No more weekly instructions. The code is how you keep going.

## Slide 9 | list

**On screen:** RULES. TOOLS. RHYTHM. RESET.

**Say:**

It has four sections.

My rules. The doctrine and boundaries you will keep. What you are building, what you protect, and the no you will say most often.

My tools. Which of the ten thinking tools you reach for, and in which situations. You will not use all ten every week. Choose by relevance. Opportunity cost before any new commitment. Incentives before any important conversation. Feedback loops every Friday. Whatever fits your life.

My rhythm. Sessions, reviews and recovery in a normal week. When you work, when you check, when you rest.

My reset. What you do the day after you slip. Because you will slip. Everyone does. The code says, in advance, what happens next.

## Slide 10 | scene

**On screen:** SIX MONTHS LATER.
**Visual:** scene: a woman at a café table on a weekday morning, a laptop and a worn operating code page in a plastic sleeve beside her coffee

**Say:**

A second case, from someone further down the road.

Sophie finished this course six months ago. Her Doom Project was a small business teaching Vietnamese cooking classes in her flat. By the end of the six weeks she had run three paid classes.

## Slide 11 | story

**On screen:** The code did not stop the drift. It brought her back.

**Say:**

By month four, her sessions had quietly drifted to one a week. There was no crisis. A busy period at her day job, a family wedding, a cold that lasted a fortnight. Just drift.

Her sixty-day review was in her calendar, so it caught it. She sat down with her operating code, and the reset section told her exactly what to do. Notice. Record. Repair. Resume.

She noticed. She wrote one line: drifted, mostly my choices, partly the job. She cut one commitment that had crept in: a committee she had agreed to join because she could not think how to refuse. She restored two sessions a week. And she booked the next review.

The code did not stop her drifting. Nothing would have. It brought her back, faster than she would ever have managed alone. That is what an operating code is for.

## Slide 12 | scene

**On screen:** THIRTY. SIXTY. NINETY.
**Visual:** scene: a phone calendar with three future dates marked in red, each labelled REVIEW, scrolling forward through the months

**Say:**

So give your plan three dated reviews, and put them in your calendar today.

At thirty days, check whether the system is still running without a course to prompt it. Are the sessions happening? Is the reset working?

At sixty days, check the evidence. What does reality say about the milestone? Use your feedback loop and your confidence statement.

At ninety days, decide. Continue, change or withdraw. Then choose the next chapter deliberately, the same way you chose this one.

## Slide 13 | red

**On screen:** BECOME DANGEROUS.

**Say:**

Become dangerous. Here is what we have meant by it all along.

Capable: you can do something real and show the evidence. Deliberate: you choose where your time, money and attention go. Hard to knock off course: you have a reset, a margin and a code. And able to choose your response when circumstances change, without waiting for permission or a prompt.

It has never meant dangerous to other people. Doom's mistake in the stories was to confuse his own judgement with a right to rule others. We left that behind in lesson one, and we leave it behind now.

## Slide 14 | scene

**On screen:** YOUR AMBITION GAVE YOU A CLAIM ON YOUR OWN LIFE.
**Visual:** scene: a man walking alone up a steep street at dawn, coat collar up, the city waking below him

**Say:**

That brings us back to where we started. Principle one: put yourself above all people.

Six weeks ago it may have sounded harsh. You know now what it means. Your flourishing gets a real vote in how your life is spent. You protect what your future needs. You deal with your responsibilities in the open. And you carry the consequences yourself.

Your ambition gave you a claim on your own life, and on nothing else. That turned out to be enough. It is exactly where the power is.

## Slide 15 | list

**On screen:** HOW PEOPLE STALL AFTER A COURSE.

**Say:**

Here is where people go wrong after a course like this.

No next step. The course ends, and so does the practice. Six weeks of momentum disappear in three.

Everything at once. Three big goals, all started, none finished. The energy of the final week turns into a plan nobody could keep.

No reviews. Nobody checks in at thirty, sixty or ninety days, including you. Put the dates in now.

And the new course. Some people finish one programme and immediately look for another, because being taught feels like progress. You do not need another course yet. You need ninety days of running your own code.

## Slide 16 | scene

**On screen:** ONE CHAPTER AT A TIME.
**Visual:** scene: a hand closing a full notebook and placing a new, empty one on top of it, a pen across the cover

**Say:**

Three things to carry out of this course.

Your time is finite, so spend it on purpose. Choose one chapter at a time, and name what stays undone. And run your code without waiting for anyone to prompt you.

## Slide 17 | steps

**On screen:** WRITE YOUR OPERATING CODE.

**Say:**

Pause the video when I tell you to. Take fifteen minutes for this one. It is the last exercise I will set you.

One. Repeat the Day 1 scenario, without looking at your first answer. Then compare the two, and write one sentence about what changed.

Two. Write your operating code. Rules, tools, rhythm and reset. One page.

Three. Choose one ninety-day milestone. Specific, and sustainable alongside the rest of your life.

Four. Name what will stay undone. Then put three review dates in your calendar: thirty, sixty and ninety days from Doomsday.

Pause now.

Your mission is workbook page W06-D05, write the operating code.

Then comes Doomsday. You demonstrate the project, defend one decision, respond to a change you did not see coming and take your next step, in front of the Council. You have rehearsed every part of it.

And on Day 42 you integrate the feedback, name the unfinished work and set your thirty, sixty and ninety-day milestones.
