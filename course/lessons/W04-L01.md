## Slide 1 | title

**On screen:** READ INTERESTS. DON'T READ MINDS.
**Visual:** hood

**Say:**

Welcome to week four: command the room.

For three weeks most of your work has happened on your own. You chose, you protected, you built and you tested. This week your project meets other people: clients, colleagues, managers, partners, the people who can say yes or no to the thing you are building.

We start with a skill almost everyone believes they have, and very few people practise. Reading what the other person actually wants.

## Slide 2 | scene

**On screen:** THE MEETING IS TOMORROW. THE STORY IS ALREADY WRITTEN.
**Visual:** scene: a man in a car park at night, engine off, talking to himself, a folder of papers on the passenger seat

**Say:**

Before a difficult conversation, most of us run a story about the other person. We rehearse it in the car, in the shower, at three in the morning.

They only care about price. They want to catch me out. They have already decided. They think I'm not good enough.

## Slide 3 | question

**On screen:** You think you know what they want. How do you know?

**Say:**

So ask yourself one question. How do you know?

Usually the honest answer is that you guessed. You took a few facts, filled the gaps with your own fears, and then walked into the room arguing with someone who exists only in your head.

## Slide 4 | scene

**On screen:** EVERYONE IS PAID FOR SOMETHING.
**Visual:** scene: a procurement manager at her desk in an open-plan office, a dashboard of red and green figures on her screen, a phone pressed to her ear

**Say:**

There is a better way. It starts from a plain fact about people. Everyone in a room is being rewarded for something, measured on something and afraid of something. Their behaviour, including the behaviour that annoys you, usually makes sense once you can see those three things.

## Slide 5 | red

**On screen:** WHAT OUTCOMES MIGHT MATTER TO EACH PARTY?

**Say:**

The fifth thinking tool is incentives. The question: what outcomes might matter to each party, and how can I check?

Notice the word "might". You do not know yet. And notice the second half of the question. How can I check? Incentives is a tool for asking better questions. It is never a licence to decide what someone else is thinking.

## Slide 6 | scene

**On screen:** REWARD. FEAR. MEASURE.
**Visual:** scene: a manager in a glass office looking at a wall chart, a single target line drawn across it in red marker

**Say:**

When you map someone's interests, look for three things.

## Slide 7 | list

**On screen:** THREE INCENTIVES TO LOOK FOR.

**Say:**

Reward. What gets this person praised, paid or promoted? A buyer might be rewarded for saving money. A manager might be rewarded for keeping the team stable.

Fear. What would get them blamed if it went wrong? Very often fear explains more than reward does. The person who blocks your proposal may simply be the one who gets the phone call when it fails.

Measure. Which number is their boss actually looking at? If their target is speed, they will trade money for time. If their target is cost, they will trade time for money.

Most behaviour that seems unreasonable makes sense once you see one of these three.

## Slide 8 | scene

**On screen:** KNOW. INFER. ASK.
**Visual:** scene: a sheet of paper on a café table divided into three columns with handwritten headings, a pen and a flat white beside it

**Say:**

You capture all of this on an interest map, and the map has three columns.

## Slide 9 | radial

**On screen:** Know. Infer. Ask.

**Say:**

Know. What they have actually said or done, that you could point to. An email. A complaint. A decision they made last year.

Infer. Your best guess about what they want. It is useful and reasonable, and it is still a guess.

Ask. The question that would turn your guess into evidence.

That middle column is where all the danger lives. The limit on this tool: an inferred motive is not a fact. Write it down, and label it honestly as a guess. The moment you forget it was a guess, you start negotiating with a ghost.

## Slide 10 | scene

**On screen:** HE ASSUMES THEY WANT A DISCOUNT.
**Visual:** scene (Winston at his kitchen table the night before, a printed contract renewal in front of him with a lower figure pencilled in and circled)

**Say:**

Here is Winston. He is an independent logistics consultant, and his biggest client's contract is up for renewal on Thursday. It is a third of his income.

He is sure they are going to push for a discount. Budgets are tight everywhere. So he has already prepared a lower price, fifteen per cent off, to offer before they ask.

## Slide 11 | story

**On screen:** Know: two complaints about turnaround. Infer: price. Ask: what matters most?

**Say:**

He runs the interest map first.

Know. They renewed last year without negotiating. They have complained twice in six months, both times about how long his reports took to arrive. Their new operations director started in the spring.

Infer. They want a lower price. Maybe the new director wants to make a mark.

Ask. What matters most to them this year? What is the new director measured on?

Already the map looks different on paper. Nothing he actually knows is about price. Everything he knows is about time.

## Slide 12 | scene

**On screen:** BEFORE WE TALK NUMBERS.
**Visual:** scene: two men across a meeting-room table, one leaning forward with an open question on his face, the other mid-answer, a window onto a loading yard behind them

**Say:**

He has three ways into the meeting.

## Slide 13 | compare

**On screen:** Three ways into the meeting

**Say:**

Act on the guess. He offers fifteen per cent off before anyone asks. They accept, happily. He has given away a large slice of a third of his income, and solved a problem they never had.

Brace for a fight. He arrives defensive. Every question sounds like an attack, so he answers sharply, and the new director leaves the meeting wondering whether he is difficult to work with.

Check the motive. He opens with a question. Before we talk numbers, what would make this year better than last year for you?

The director says speed, without hesitating. They are measured on how quickly they respond to problems in the warehouse, and Winston's reports arrive too late to help.

So Winston proposes a faster turnaround at the same price, and a premium tier for urgent same-day work. They take both. He walks out with more income, not less, because he asked one question before he opened his mouth about money.

## Slide 14 | scene

**On screen:** DEVELOP YOUR KILLER INSTINCT.
**Visual:** scene: a boxer in a quiet gym watching an opponent's footwork from the corner, perfectly still

**Say:**

That is where this week's principle comes in.

## Slide 15 | red

**On screen:** SEE THE OPENING. THEN MOVE.

**Say:**

Principle three says: develop your killer instinct.

In this course it means acting decisively on a real opportunity, and being willing to hear no. The opening in Winston's meeting was speed. Once he saw it, he moved fast and asked clearly for more.

But you can only act on an opening you can see. Killer instinct is the decisive move that follows good reading. It has nothing to do with aggression, intimidation or charging in. The boxer watches before he strikes.

## Slide 16 | scene

**On screen:** SHE PREPARED FOR A FLAT NO.
**Visual:** scene: a woman in a hotel function room after an event, chairs being stacked around her, a manager walking over with a clipboard

**Say:**

A second case.

Chloe is an events coordinator at a hotel group. She wants a raise. She expects a flat no, because the company has had a poor year, and she has prepared a long argument about her workload.

## Slide 17 | story

**On screen:** One question found what the argument would have missed.

**Say:**

She runs the map. Know: the company had a poor year. Her team lead is going on parental leave in two months. Infer: no money for raises. Ask: what is my manager most worried about this year?

So instead of opening with the argument, she asks one question. What would make my role more valuable to you this year?

Her manager's real worry is cover for the team lead. Nobody has been found, and the manager will be blamed if events go wrong while she is away.

They agree that Chloe takes on the team lead's responsibilities for the leave period, with a raise tied to it and a review when the lead returns. All of it in writing. The question found an interest the argument would have missed completely.

## Slide 18 | scene

**On screen:** A GUESS IS NOT A FACT.
**Visual:** scene: a handwritten note stuck to a laptop lid reading "Guess ≠ fact. Ask."

**Say:**

Keep this one on the wall. A guess is not a fact.

Your instinct is valuable when it points you towards the right question. It becomes dangerous the moment it replaces the question.

## Slide 19 | list

**On screen:** MISREADING THE ROOM.

**Say:**

Here is where people go wrong.

Projection. They assume the other person wants what they would want. Winston worries about money, so he assumed his client did too.

Interrogation. They learn that questions are powerful and fire them one after another until the meeting feels like a cross-examination. Two or three good questions are plenty.

Not listening. They ask, then wait for their turn to talk, already rehearsing their answer. The answer is the whole point of the question.

And manipulation. Some people learn incentives and start using what they discover against people, pressing on fears they uncovered. That burns trust and it violates the spirit of this course. Find the interest so you can offer something that serves both of you.

## Slide 20 | scene

**On screen:** SEPARATE WHAT YOU KNOW FROM WHAT YOU GUESS.
**Visual:** scene: two people shaking hands at the door of a small office, both smiling, a folder under one arm

**Say:**

Three things to keep.

Separate what you know from what you infer, and label your guesses honestly. Look for reward, fear and measure. And ask a real question, then actually listen to the answer.

## Slide 21 | steps

**On screen:** MAP THE INTERESTS.

**Say:**

Pause the video when I tell you to.

One. Choose a real conversation you will have in the next week. Ideally one that matters to your Doom Project. A client, a manager, a partner, a supplier.

Two. Write what you know about the other person's interests. Only things you could point to.

Three. Write what you are inferring. Their likely reward, fear and measure. Label all of it as a guess.

Four. Write three questions that would test those guesses. Open questions, the kind that cannot be answered with yes or no.

Pause now and give it five minutes.
