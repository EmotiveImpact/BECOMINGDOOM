Welcome to week three: enter the laboratory.

Two weeks in, you have a doctrine, a first no, a priced calendar, a room built for work and a reset for the bad days. That is the fortress. Now the question changes. What can you actually do?

## Slide 2 | scene

**On screen:** THREE WEEKENDS. STILL NOT LIVE.
**Visual:** scene: a man hunched over a laptop at a desk covered in camera gear, a half-built website on screen full of placeholder boxes, a Sunday-evening window behind him

**Say:**

Most projects do not fail because the person was lazy. They stall under the weight of things they never needed.

The custom website. The perfect logo. The course that has to cover everything before anyone is allowed to see it. The business plan with a five-year forecast for a business with no customers yet.

## Slide 3 | question

**On screen:** What does it actually need?

**Say:**

So before you build anything else this week, ask one question. What does this project actually need in order to do its job?

Leave aside what would be nice and what other people's projects seem to have. Find what it needs.

## Slide 4 | scene

**On screen:** BORROWED REQUIREMENTS.
**Visual:** scene: a man scrolling a competitor's polished website on his phone on a bus, his own reflection faint in the window

**Say:**

The trouble is that most of our requirements are borrowed. We look at someone further along, notice everything they have, and assume we need all of it before we can begin.

But you are seeing the result of years, with no way to tell which parts actually do the work and which they added later because they could.

## Slide 5 | red

**On screen:** REQUIRED. OBSERVED. OR MERELY ASSUMED?

**Say:**

The third thinking tool is first principles. The question: what is required, what is observed, and what is merely assumed?

Required means the project fails without it. If you removed it, the thing would stop doing its job.

Observed means you have seen real evidence for it. Someone told you, showed you or paid you.

Assumed means you believe it, and you have not checked. Most of the plan usually lives here.

## Slide 7 | list

**On screen:** THE JOB. THE SORT. THE CUT.

**Say:**

The job. Write the single job the project must do, for one specific person. Help a couple choose a wedding photographer. Get six people to a trial class. One sentence.

The sort. Go through every item in your plan and mark it required, observed or assumed. Be strict. If you cannot point to the evidence, it is assumed.

The cut. Park everything assumed. Parked, not deleted. Write it on a list called later. It comes back the moment a test shows it matters.

What remains after the cut is usually much smaller than you expected, and much closer to finished.

## Slide 8 | scene

**On screen:** A CUSTOM SITE FROM SCRATCH.
**Visual:** scene (Tom at his desk, the half-built site on one screen and a folder of his best wedding photographs on the other)

**Say:**

Here is Tom. He is a wedding and events photographer. He is good, and he wants to move up from cheap local jobs to better-paid weddings.

His Doom Project is a new website. He has spent three weekends building it from scratch with a custom gallery, animated menus and a booking system. It is still not live. No couple has seen any of it.

## Slide 9 | story

**On screen:** Clients need evidence they can assess.

**Say:**

He runs first principles.

The job. Help an engaged couple decide whether to contact me.

The sort. What does a couple actually need in order to decide? They need to see work like their wedding, done well. They need to know roughly what it costs. They need a way to get in touch. Those three are required.

Is the custom gallery required? He assumes so. The animated menus? Assumed. The booking system? Couples usually want a conversation before they book a photographer, so he is not even sure it helps. Assumed.

## Slide 11 | compare

**On screen:** Three ways forward

**Say:**

Keep building. A fourth weekend on the site. It looks better every week. Still no couple has seen anything.

Copy a rival. He takes the structure of a well-known photographer's site. It looks professional, but he has no idea which parts are doing the work, so when it does not bring enquiries he has nothing to learn from.

Strip it back. A simple page with three full weddings, a starting price and a contact button, live by Friday on an ordinary website builder. Then he tests the assumption that matters most: that these three weddings are the right ones. He shows the page to two recently engaged couples, friends of friends, and asks which wedding would make them get in touch.

Both pick the same one, and it is not the one Tom thought was his best. That single answer changes his whole portfolio. He would never have learned it from a fourth weekend of building.

## Slide 12 | scene

**On screen:** SOME CONVENTIONS EXIST FOR A REASON.
**Visual:** scene: an old master craftsman's workshop, hand tools hung in neat rows on a pegboard, a younger man watching him work

**Say:**

Now the limit on this tool, because first principles is easy to misuse.

Do not throw away accumulated expertise without a reason. Some conventions exist because they work. Photographers show full weddings because couples want to see a whole day, not just highlights. Accountants use certain formats because regulators require them.

First principles leaves room for what others have learned. It asks you to know why something is there before you copy it, and to be honest when you do not know.

## Slide 13 | story

**On screen:** A room. A time. Six people. A way to pay.

**Say:**

A second case.

Zara wants to teach yoga. She has trained for two years. Her plan included a studio lease, a brand, an app, a ten-week programme and professional photographs, all before anyone had signed up. It would cost thousands and take months. So she had not started.

Stripped back, the requirements of a yoga class were a room, a time, some people and a way to pay. Everything else was assumed.

She books a community hall for one Saturday morning at fifteen pounds. She tells her friends, posts once in a local group and asks everyone to bring someone. Nine people come. Four book the next one before they leave.

Now she has evidence. Some of her assumptions, like the brand, might matter later, and she can test them then. The studio can wait.

## Slide 14 | scene

**On screen:** WHAT THE EXTRA FEATURE REALLY COSTS.
**Visual:** scene: a calendar on a laptop showing three weekends blocked "Website build" and an empty column headed "Client conversations"

**Say:**

Bring back the tool from week two. Opportunity cost.

Every feature you add has a price. Tom's three weekends on the site cost him three weekends of conversations with couples. Zara's months of planning cost her months of teaching, which is the only thing that would ever have made her a better teacher.

When you are about to add something, ask what it pushes out. Very often the answer is contact with real people.

## Slide 15 | red

**On screen:** VOW TO BECOME A MASTER.

**Say:**

That is why this week's principle sits here. Principle seven says: vow to become a master.

Mastery is a long commitment to feedback-informed skill. It is built in contact with the people who use your work. Polishing in private feels like progress, because you are busy and things are getting prettier. But nobody becomes a master in a room with no feedback.

And be honest about the timescale. Six weeks will not make you a master of anything. Six weeks can make you someone who has started properly, with real feedback and a record of improvement. That is the vow.

## Slide 17 | list

**On screen:** CUT SIZE. KEEP QUALITY.

**Say:**

Cutting quality. They remove what makes the work good instead of what makes it big. Tom stripped the menus, not the quality of his photographs. Make it smaller, never worse.

Fake observed. One friend's opinion gets promoted to evidence. Your mum liking it is lovely. Market research it isn't.

Never rebuilding. A test shows something genuinely matters, and they refuse to add it because they have fallen in love with being minimal. Minimal is a starting point, never a religion.

And the clever rebel. They use first principles as an excuse to ignore every expert and reinvent everything. That is a very slow way to rediscover what everyone already knew.

## Slide 18 | scene

Three things to keep.

Name the one job the project must do. Required beats assumed, so build the required parts first. And test the assumption that matters most before you build anything else around it.

## Slide 19 | steps

**On screen:** BUILD YOUR REQUIREMENTS SHEET.

**Say:**

Pause the video when I tell you to.

One. Write the single job your Doom Project must do, for one specific person.

Four. Circle the assumption that matters most: the one that would change your plan if it turned out to be wrong. Then write the quickest honest test for it. A question to two people. A one-day trial. A simple page.
