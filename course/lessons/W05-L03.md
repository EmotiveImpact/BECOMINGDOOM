## Slide 1 | title

**On screen:** MAKE THE OFFER.
**Visual:** hood

**Say:**

You know your audience, their problem in their own words, your lever and your signal. Now you put something in front of them and ask them to commit.

This is the lesson where the empire meets the market. For many of you it is also the moment the whole course has been quietly preparing you for: asking for something, clearly, and being ready for any answer.

## Slide 2 | scene

**On screen:** "LET ME KNOW IF I CAN HELP."
**Visual:** scene: a social media post on a phone screen reading "Happy to help anyone who needs it! Just reach out", with no comments under it

**Say:**

Listen to how most people offer their work.

Let me know if I can help. Happy to chat any time. Reach out if you ever need anything. Open for work.

## Slide 3 | question

**On screen:** What exactly are they saying yes to?

**Say:**

These sound generous. And they nearly always produce nothing at all.

The reason is simple. They ask for nothing and they offer nothing specific. The person reading them would have to work out what you do, what it costs, how long it takes and what they should do next. Most people will not do that work. They scroll on.

## Slide 4 | scene

**On screen:** A REAL OFFER HAS EDGES.
**Visual:** scene: a chalkboard menu outside a small café listing three dishes, each with a price, under a red awning

**Say:**

Think about the last thing you bought from a small business. The café menu outside the door. The price on the barber's wall. You knew what you were getting, what it cost, and what to do next.

A real offer has edges. The result someone will get. The scope: what is included and what is not. The time it takes. The cost, in money or effort. And the specific commitment you are asking for.

When an offer has edges, people can say yes to it. Or no, which is also useful.

## Slide 5 | red

**On screen:** SMALL AND CLEAR BEATS BIG AND VAGUE.

**Say:**

Here is the rule for this week. A small, clear offer beats a big, vague promise.

You can always expand a pilot that works. You cannot easily shrink a promise you made to everyone. Start with something small enough to deliver brilliantly, clear enough to say yes to, and bounded enough that both sides know when it ends.

## Slide 6 | scene

**On screen:** FIVE PARTS. ONE PAGE.
**Visual:** scene: a single printed offer sheet on a workbench, five short sections, a pencil and a tape measure lying across it

**Say:**

A complete offer has five parts, and it fits on one page.

## Slide 7 | list

**On screen:** RESULT. SCOPE. TIME. COST. ASK.

**Say:**

The result, in their words. What will be different for them afterwards? Use the language your audience used when they described the problem.

The scope. What is in, and just as important, what is out.

The time. A start and an end. When does it begin, how long does it run, when do they get the result?

The cost. Stated plainly, on the page, even if it is free. Free still costs their time and attention, so say how much.

And the ask. The exact thing you want them to do next. Book a call this week. Reply yes. Pay the deposit. Fill in the form.

If any of the five is missing, the person reading has to guess. Every guess is a reason to do nothing.

## Slide 8 | scene

**On screen:** "I CAN HELP ANY BUSINESS GET ORGANISED."
**Visual:** scene (Kofi in a small engineering workshop, tools hanging on a wall system he built, a cluttered bench at the other end of the room)

**Say:**

Here is Kofi. He is a joiner, and over the last few years he has designed a storage system for small workshops: wall panels, tool racks and labelled bins that make a cramped space work. His own workshop is the proof. His Doom Project is selling it to other businesses.

His first instinct is to post that he can help any local business get organised.

## Slide 9 | compare

**On screen:** Three ways to make the offer

**Say:**

He has three ways to make the offer.

The open promise. I can help any business with anything to do with storage. It sounds big and capable, and nobody replies, because nobody knows what they would be agreeing to or what it would cost.

Give it all away. He offers to sort out a friend's garage workshop for free, with no end date. By week three he is still there every Saturday, the friend keeps adding jobs, and Kofi resents the whole thing. The friend has no reason to value the work, because it cost nothing and has no edges.

The bounded pilot. Four weeks, one workshop. He measures, designs and fits one wall system. Fixed fee, half up front. And a review on day twenty-eight where they decide together whether to do the rest of the space.

## Slide 10 | scene

**On screen:** FOUR WEEKS. ONE WALL. ONE FEE. ONE REVIEW.
**Visual:** scene: Kofi shaking hands with the owner of a small motorbike repair shop in front of a newly fitted tool wall

**Say:**

He sends the offer to six small workshops he already knows: a motorbike repair shop, a bike mechanic, two joiners, a small printer and a car body shop.

Two say no, politely. One does not reply. Two say they are interested but not this month. The motorbike shop says yes and pays the deposit that week.

One yes out of six. Count what it gives him: a first client, a fitted wall he can photograph, and four other businesses who now know exactly what he does.

## Slide 11 | red

**On screen:** THE OFFER HOLDS THE LINE.

**Say:**

Look at how much of the earlier course is inside that offer.

Week four: the pilot holds the line on price. Kofi did not discount to win. He reduced the scope instead.

The review date is a planned decision point, so either side can continue or withdraw cleanly. That is the tactical withdrawal, built in before anyone needs it.

Second-order consequences: a fixed fee and a clear end mean he is not setting up years of unpaid Saturdays.

And feedback loops: the review on day twenty-eight is his signal. Did the wall get used? Did the owner want more?

Principle three, develop your killer instinct, is the part that takes nerve. Asking for the deposit. Making a clear request, and being ready to hear no.

## Slide 12 | scene

**On screen:** "COMMISSIONS OPEN. MESSAGE ME."
**Visual:** scene: an illustrator at a drawing tablet in a small bedroom studio, a pet portrait half finished on screen, a cat asleep on the desk

**Say:**

A second case.

Leah is an illustrator. She posted that her commissions were open, and to message her. Six likes. No orders.

## Slide 13 | story

**On screen:** Pet portraits. A4. Two weeks. Eighty pounds. Five slots.

**Say:**

Nobody knew what to ask for, what size, what style or what it would cost. So nobody asked.

Her new offer had edges. Pet portraits, in her style, A4 digital print. Two-week turnaround. Eighty pounds. Five slots this month. Send a photo and the deposit to book.

Four slots were booked in two days. And one person asked whether she could do A3, for a larger price. That question gave her the next offer to test.

Same artist, same skill, same audience. The only difference was the edges.

## Slide 14 | scene

**On screen:** EVERY RESPONSE COUNTS.
**Visual:** scene: a notebook page with three columns, YES, NO and SILENCE, each with names and short notes underneath

**Say:**

Every response to your offer counts as evidence.

A yes is a commitment to deliver, so deliver well. A no is information about fit, price or timing. Write down why, if they told you. Silence gets one polite follow-up after a few days, and then it is recorded as data too.

## Slide 15 | red

**On screen:** YOUR PASS NEVER DEPENDS ON THEIR YES.

**Say:**

Here is something important. Your pass in this course never depends on someone saying yes.

It depends on making a clear offer to the people it is for, and learning honestly from what happens. A clean offer that gets six polite refusals and teaches you why is a far better result than a vague post that gets nothing and teaches you nothing.

And your offer does not have to involve money. A free pilot, a participation invitation, a release of something you made with a clear request attached: all of these count. What matters is that it has edges and an ask.

## Slide 16 | scene

**On screen:** OFFERS NOBODY CAN ACCEPT.
**Visual:** scene: a beautifully designed flyer on a shop counter with no price and no contact details, a customer turning it over looking for them

**Say:**

Here is where people go wrong.

## Slide 17 | list

**On screen:** NO PRICE. NO EDGES. NO ASK.

**Say:**

No price. Making people ask how much. Most will not. They will assume it is too expensive and move on.

No edges. Unlimited scope that turns into resentment by week three, for you and often for them.

No ask. A lovely description of what you do that ends without telling anyone what to do next.

And the hidden offer. You make the offer once, quietly, to two people, and then decide nobody wanted it. Put it in front of enough of the right people that the answer actually means something.

## Slide 18 | scene

**On screen:** GIVE IT EDGES. END WITH THE ASK.
**Visual:** scene: a man pressing Send on an email titled "Workshop wall pilot: four weeks, one fee", his other hand flat on the bench

**Say:**

Three things to keep.

Small and clear beats big and vague. Give the offer edges: result, scope, time, cost and ask. And always end with the ask.

## Slide 19 | steps

**On screen:** BUILD YOUR OFFER SHEET.

**Say:**

Pause the video when I tell you to.

One. The result, in one sentence your audience would use.

Two. The scope. What is included, and what is not.

Three. The time and the cost, stated plainly. Including free, if it is free.

Four. The exact commitment you will ask for, and who you will send it to. Name at least five people or places.

Then read it as if you were a stranger. Could you say yes to this in under a minute? If not, fix the part that made you hesitate.

Pause now and give it ten minutes.

## Production notes

- No income claims. Kofi's and Leah's prices and response rates are illustrative, and participants set their own terms.

- Delivery: the ask lines should be spoken plainly and without apology.
