# W03-L02: Invert the failure

## Slide 1 | title

**On screen:** INVERT THE FAILURE.
**Visual:** hood

**Say:**

Yesterday you stripped your project back to what it truly requires. Today we look at it from the other end.

Most planning asks how to succeed. Today we turn the question upside down and ask how this project is going to fail.

It sounds pessimistic. In practice it is one of the fastest ways to make something work, and it is how serious people in dangerous professions think every day.

## Slide 2 | scene

**On screen:** DOOMSDAY. THE DEMO IS DEAD.
**Visual:** scene: a man standing at the front of a small room beside a projector screen showing a frozen error message, faces in the audience waiting

**Say:**

Imagine it is Doomsday, the end of week six. You stand up to show your project, and it does not work. Something has gone badly wrong.

## Slide 3 | question

**On screen:** What killed it?

**Say:**

What killed it?

Answer quickly, in your head, right now. Your first three answers are usually the most useful, because they are the ones you already half know and have been avoiding.

## Slide 4 | scene

**On screen:** PILOTS CHECK. SURGEONS COUNT.
**Visual:** scene: two pilots in a cockpit running through a printed checklist before take-off, morning light on the instruments

**Say:**

Pilots run checklists before every flight, however many thousands of hours they have flown. Surgical teams count instruments before they close. They do this because they have learned to ask what is most likely to go wrong, and to make it impossible before it starts.

You do not need their stakes to use their thinking.

## Slide 5 | red

**On screen:** WHAT WOULD MAKE THIS FAIL, AND WHAT CAN I PREVENT?

**Say:**

The fourth thinking tool is inversion. The question: what would make this fail, and what can I prevent?

Pay attention to the second half of that question. Inversion is never just a list of fears. Every risk ends with an action. A risk without an action is only a worry with better handwriting.

## Slide 6 | scene

**On screen:** FAIL IT ON PURPOSE.
**Visual:** scene: a notebook page headed "Doomsday: it failed because..." with three numbered lines filled in

**Say:**

The most useful form of inversion is called a pre-mortem. A post-mortem asks why something died after it is already dead. A pre-mortem asks the same question while you can still do something about it.

## Slide 7 | list

**On screen:** SET THE SCENE. LIST THE CAUSES. RANK AND ACT.

**Say:**

It runs in three steps.

Set the scene. It is Doomsday and the project has failed. Write as if it has already happened. That small trick makes you more honest, because you are explaining a past event instead of defending a plan.

List the causes. Fast and specific. Technical failures, personal ones, practical ones. Ten minutes, no more.

Rank and act. Pick the three most likely causes and give each one a preventive action with a date beside it.

## Slide 8 | scene

**On screen:** HOW PROJECTS DIE.
**Visual:** scene: a broken link page on a phone screen held up in a pub, the person holding it shrugging

**Say:**

You will find that projects tend to die in a few familiar ways.

## Slide 9 | list

**On screen:** NOBODY CAN OPEN IT. NOBODY ASKED FOR IT. NOBODY FINISHED IT.

**Say:**

Nobody can open it. A broken link. The wrong permissions. A file that only works on your laptop. A demo that needs wifi in a room without any.

Nobody asked for it. It was built for an audience that turned out not to exist, or that wanted something slightly different.

Nobody finished it. The scope kept growing faster than the hours available, until week six arrived with ninety per cent of four things and one hundred per cent of none.

Each of these is preventable. Each is far cheaper to prevent than to fix on the day.

## Slide 10 | scene

**On screen:** ONE MORE FEATURE. OR CHECK IF ANYONE CAN OPEN IT.
**Visual:** scene (Omar at a desk with two monitors, code on one, a half-finished dashboard on the other, a phone and a tablet lying unused beside him)

**Say:**

Here is Omar. He does IT support for small offices, and his Doom Project is a simple dashboard that shows each client the state of their devices and backups. He is excited about it. This week he wants to add one more feature: automatic alerts.

He runs inversion first. How would this fail at Doomsday?

The answer comes immediately. The client cannot open it. He has only ever tested it on his own laptop, logged in as himself.

## Slide 11 | compare

**On screen:** Three uses of the same hour

**Say:**

He has one free hour this evening. Three ways to spend it.

Add the feature. More to show on Doomsday, and exactly the same chance of showing nothing at all.

Write a list of fears. He writes twelve risks in a document, feels anxious, and closes the laptop. No actions. That is anxiety with a spreadsheet.

Prevent the likeliest failure. He tests the dashboard on his phone, a tablet and his sister's laptop, logged in as a test client. He finds two problems. The page breaks on a phone, and a client account can see a menu it should not. Either one would have ended the demo, and the second would have been a real security problem for a real client.

He fixes both. Next week he adds the alerts.

## Slide 12 | scene

**On screen:** RISK LISTING CAN BECOME AVOIDANCE.
**Visual:** scene: a man staring at a long spreadsheet of risks late at night, the cell cursor blinking, nothing else done

**Say:**

That second option is the limit on this tool. Risk listing can become avoidance.

Some people love inversion because it feels responsible. They can spend a whole evening imagining disasters and never build anything. That is fear dressed up as planning. Ten minutes of pre-mortem, three risks, three actions. Then back to work.

## Slide 13 | story

**On screen:** The laptop failed. The stick worked.

**Say:**

A second case.

Ben works in a council housing office, and he has been asked to give a fifteen-minute talk at a regional conference. It is his first. His Doom Project is a guide to a new tenancy process, and the talk is how it gets seen.

He runs the pre-mortem. Doomsday, it failed because: the slides would not load on the venue's laptop. He ran fifteen minutes over and was cut off. He blanked on the opening and never recovered.

Preventions, with dates. Slides on a memory stick and in his email, by Wednesday. Two timed rehearsals with a colleague, Thursday and Friday. The first two sentences memorised word for word.

On the day the venue laptop will not read his file. The stick works on the second machine. He knows his opening cold, so the shaky first minute holds. He finishes a minute early. Nobody in the room knows how close it came. That is what good prevention looks like from the outside. Nothing happens.

## Slide 14 | scene

**On screen:** WHAT ONE SKILL WOULD MAKE THIS BETTER?
**Visual:** scene: a young woman recording herself on a phone propped against books, practising a thirty-second explanation

**Say:**

Inversion has a second use this week. You are going to choose one subskill to practise with feedback, and inversion helps you choose it.

Ask: which weakness of mine is most likely to hurt this project? Not the skill you would most enjoy practising. The one most likely to cost you.

## Slide 15 | red

**On screen:** VOW TO BECOME A MASTER.

**Say:**

That is principle seven in action. Vow to become a master. Mastery starts with deliberate practice on one small thing, with someone or something telling you how you did.

Choose a subskill small enough to practise twice this week. Writing a clearer headline. Explaining your offer in thirty seconds. Testing on a second device. Answering the most common objection. Set a baseline, practise, get feedback and compare.

## Slide 16 | scene

**On screen:** INVERSION GONE WRONG.
**Visual:** scene: a man lying awake at night, eyes open, the clock reading 3.12

**Say:**

Here is where people go wrong.

## Slide 17 | list

**On screen:** THE DOOM LOOP. VAGUE RISKS. NO DATES.

**Say:**

The doom loop. You imagine failure until you no longer want to start. Inversion is a ten-minute tool. If it is keeping you awake, you have stopped using it and started being used by it.

Vague risks. "It might not work" cannot be prevented. Name the mechanism. The login fails for new users. The talk runs long because the middle section has no time limit.

No dates. A preventive action with no time attached is just a worry written down neatly.

And preventing the wrong thing. Some people work hard to prevent the most frightening failure instead of the most likely one. Omar could have spent the evening on server outages, which are rare. The client failing to log in was common, and boring, and fatal.

## Slide 18 | scene

**On screen:** KNOW HOW IT DIES.
**Visual:** scene: a memory stick and a printed set of slides in a jacket pocket, a conference lanyard hanging out

**Say:**

Three things to keep.

Imagine the failure first, while you can still change it. Every risk gets an action and a date. And prevent the most likely failure before the most dramatic one.

## Slide 19 | steps

**On screen:** RUN YOUR PRE-MORTEM.

**Say:**

Pause the video when I tell you to.

One. Write the scene. It is Doomsday and my project failed because...

Two. List every cause you can think of in five minutes. Fast and specific.

Three. Choose the three most likely. Give each one a preventive action you can actually take.

Four. Put a date beside each action, and put the first one in your calendar this week.

Then, at the bottom of the page, write the one subskill most likely to hurt the project, and how you will get feedback on it.

Pause now and give it ten minutes.

Know how it dies. Then don't let it. Prevention is the cheapest fix you will ever make.
