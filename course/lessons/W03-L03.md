## Slide 1 | title

**On screen:** BUILD YOUR FIRST DOOMBOT.
**Visual:** hood

**Say:**

In the stories, Doom builds machines to carry out his will. Doombots. Copies of himself that handle the dangerous and the repetitive while he does the work only he can do.

Today you build your first one. It will be much less dramatic than the comics and far more useful.

## Slide 2 | scene

**On screen:** THE SAME EMAIL. THE TWELFTH TIME.
**Visual:** scene: a woman at a laptop at nine at night, the same email template half typed for the twelfth time, a stack of client folders beside her

**Say:**

Look back at the last two weeks. What did you do more than three times?

Writing nearly the same email. Formatting the same report. Copying figures from one place to another. Checking the same list before every session. Answering the same question from different people.

## Slide 3 | question

**On screen:** What could a machine carry?

**Say:**

That repetition is where your first Doombot lives.

Every hour you spend on work that is the same every time is an hour you are not spending on the work only you can do: the thinking, the judgement, the relationships, the craft. So the question is simple. Which part of your week could a machine carry?

## Slide 4 | scene

**On screen:** A TEMPLATE IS A MACHINE.
**Visual:** scene: a laminated pre-shift checklist clipped to the wall of a commercial kitchen, a chef ticking it with a marker

**Say:**

When people hear "machine" they think of software, or artificial intelligence. Sometimes it is. Very often the best machine is much simpler. A template is a machine. A checklist is a machine. A spreadsheet with three formulas is a machine.

## Slide 5 | red

**On screen:** A BOUNDED AID YOU CAN REPEAT AND CHECK.

**Say:**

So here is the definition we will use. A Doombot is a bounded, repeatable aid that you can check. Three words do the work.

Bounded. It does one defined job, and you know exactly where that job starts and stops.

Repeatable. It works the same way next week, and the week after.

Checkable. You can tell, quickly and reliably, whether its output is right.

It can be a template, a checklist, a saved prompt, a spreadsheet or a small automation. The technology matters far less than the boundary and the check.

## Slide 6 | scene

**On screen:** FREQUENT. STRUCTURED. CHECKABLE. LOW STAKES.
**Visual:** scene: a whiteboard in a small office with four words written in a column, one of them circled

**Say:**

Not every task makes a good first Doombot. A good first job has four qualities.

## Slide 7 | list

**On screen:** A GOOD FIRST DOOMBOT.

**Say:**

Frequent. You do it at least weekly, so the saving adds up quickly.

Structured. The shape is the same each time and only the details change. A weekly report. An invoice. A booking confirmation.

Checkable. You can tell in a minute or two whether the output is right.

Low stakes. A mistake costs you minutes, not a client. Start there. You earn the right to automate bigger things by running smaller ones well.

And use the tools from this week. First principles asks what this task actually requires. Inversion asks how the Doombot could fail, and what you can prevent.

## Slide 8 | scene

**On screen:** SAME REPORT. EVERY FRIDAY.
**Visual:** scene (Mei at a desk on a Friday afternoon, three client reports open in separate windows, each with the same headings)

**Say:**

Here is Mei. She is a freelance marketing consultant with five regular clients. Every Friday she writes each of them a progress report: what was done this week, the results, and what comes next.

Each report takes about two hours. That is ten hours a week, and most of the structure is identical every time.

## Slide 9 | story

**On screen:** A helper drafts. She checks.

**Say:**

Her Doom Project is to free up one day a week for winning bigger clients, and the reports are the obvious place to start.

She builds a draft-report helper. It is two things. A fixed template with her headings and her house style. And a saved prompt for an AI writing tool, which turns her rough bullet-point notes into a first draft in that template.

Then she has to decide how to use it.

## Slide 10 | scene

**On screen:** TRUST IT. IGNORE IT. OR CHECK IT.
**Visual:** scene: a split screen of a laptop, a drafted report on the left and Mei's source spreadsheet on the right, a red pen hovering over a figure

**Say:**

She has three options.

## Slide 11 | compare

**On screen:** Three ways to use the helper

**Say:**

Trust it completely. She sends whatever it writes. It is fast, right up until the week it invents a figure, or states last month's result as this month's, and a client spots it. One wrong number can cost more trust than a year of good reports earned.

Never build it. She keeps writing every report by hand. Ten hours a week, for as long as she does this work. Safe, and slowly expensive.

Draft, then verify. The helper produces a draft in ten minutes. She checks every figure against her source data, line by line, and edits the wording so it sounds like her. Forty minutes instead of two hours. Across five clients, that is around six hours back every week.

## Slide 12 | scene

**On screen:** PRIVATE DATA STAYS PRIVATE.
**Visual:** scene: a hand replacing a client's company name with "Client A" in a document before pasting it into a tool

**Say:**

And Mei has one rule she never breaks.

Private client information does not go into any tool unless she knows exactly where it is stored, who can see it, and that her client agreement allows it. When in doubt, she anonymises. Client names become Client A. Personal details come out completely.

This matters more than any time saving. Your clients trust you with their information. A Doombot that leaks it has betrayed that trust on your behalf.

## Slide 13 | story

**On screen:** Ninety minutes on Sunday night. Ten minutes now.

**Say:**

A second case, with no artificial intelligence involved at all.

Tunde runs a small cleaning firm. Six cleaners, about twenty regular jobs. Every Sunday night he spends ninety minutes matching people to jobs by hand, juggling postcodes, hours, who has a car and which client has asked not to have a particular person back.

His Doombot is a spreadsheet. One tab lists the jobs, one lists the cleaners, and a few simple rules match them: postcode area, available hours, and a column of who cannot work where.

It drafts the rota in seconds. Tunde reviews it in ten minutes and fixes two clashes the rules missed. He gets eighty minutes of his Sunday back, every Sunday. The spreadsheet carries the repetition. Tunde keeps the judgement, because only he knows that one cleaner's mother is unwell this week.

## Slide 14 | scene

**On screen:** COMPARE IT WITH THE MANUAL VERSION.
**Visual:** scene: two printed versions of the same rota side by side on a table, differences circled in red

**Say:**

Now, the test. Do not decide a Doombot works because it feels clever.

Run it on one bounded task where you already know the right answer. Do the same task manually. Then compare the two against an explicit rubric, written in advance. Usually three lines: accuracy, time taken, and how much editing it needed.

## Slide 15 | red

**On screen:** KEEP. CHANGE. OR DISCARD.

**Say:**

Then decide. Keep it, change it, or discard it.

Discarding is a perfectly good result. You learned cheaply that this job is not suitable, and you can try the next one.

This is also the first glimpse of principle five, build your empire. Empires are built on systems that keep working when you are not watching every step. A Doombot is the smallest possible version of that: one piece of your work that now runs on a system, with you still in charge of the quality.

## Slide 16 | scene

**On screen:** BOTS THAT BITE.
**Visual:** scene: an invoice on a laptop screen with the wrong client's name at the top, a hand pressed to a forehead

**Say:**

Here is where people go wrong.

## Slide 17 | list

**On screen:** TOO BROAD. NO CHECK. PRIVATE DATA.

**Say:**

Too broad. "Do my admin" is not a job. "Draft the weekly invoice from these notes" is a job. If you cannot describe where it starts and stops, it is not bounded.

No check. Sending output nobody has verified, until the day it is wrong in front of the person who matters most.

Private data. Pasting client details, health information or anyone's personal records into a tool you do not control. Strip the names out, or do not use the tool for that job.

And automating the mess. If the manual process is confused, a Doombot will repeat the confusion faster. Fix the process on paper first. Then build the machine.

## Slide 18 | scene

**On screen:** THE MACHINE CARRIES. YOU JUDGE.
**Visual:** scene: a man closing a laptop on a Sunday evening and walking into a lit kitchen where his family is eating

**Say:**

Three things to keep.

One bounded job, chosen because it is frequent, structured, checkable and low stakes. Always check the output against a rubric before it reaches anyone else. And keep the judgement for yourself.

## Slide 19 | steps

**On screen:** DESIGN YOUR DOOMBOT.

**Say:**

Pause the video when I tell you to.

One. Name the repeated task it will carry. One sentence, with a clear start and stop.

Two. Choose the simplest form that could do it. Template, checklist, spreadsheet, saved prompt or automation.

Three. Write the three-line rubric you will use to check its output.

Four. List what it must never touch. Private data, final decisions, anything you cannot verify.

Pause now and give it five minutes.
