## Slide 2 | scene

**On screen:** THE SESSION STARTED AT EIGHT. THE WORK STARTED AT NINE.
**Visual:** scene: a young man at a desk at night, face lit by a phone held below the edge of the desk, an untouched document on the monitor

**Say:**

Think about your last work session.

You sat down on time. You opened the laptop. Then you checked one message. Then one more thing. Then you went to make a drink, came back, and remembered something you needed to look up.

## Slide 3 | question

**On screen:** How many times did your phone win the last session?

**Say:**

How many times did something else win? A notification. A message. A quick check that turned into twenty minutes.

Most people blame themselves for this. They call it a lack of discipline and promise to try harder next time. Then next time they sit in the same room, with the same phone, with the same notifications on, and get the same result.

Every one of those interruptions was a gate left open. Discipline is far easier when the room is on your side.

## Slide 4 | scene

**On screen:** THE ROOM DECIDES BEFORE YOU DO.
**Visual:** scene: a cluttered desk seen from above: phone, three cups, open post, a game controller, a laptop squeezed into the middle

**Say:**

Your surroundings make thousands of small decisions for you before you get the chance to. A phone within reach gets checked. A first task that is vague gets postponed. A desk covered in other things invites other things.

So this lesson is about building the conditions for execution instead of relying on willpower in the moment.

## Slide 5 | red

**On screen:** WHAT CAN I DECIDE, INFLUENCE OR ONLY RESPOND TO?

**Say:**

The second thinking tool is control and influence. The question: what can I decide, what can I influence, and what can I only respond to?

You take everything that touches your session and sort it into those three boxes. Then you put your effort where it actually moves something.

It is a sorting heuristic, a practical habit, not a law of nature. But used honestly it ends two of the most common wastes in any working life: fighting things you cannot change, and ignoring things you could.

## Slide 6 | scene

**On screen:** DECIDE. INFLUENCE. RESPOND.
**Visual:** scene: a front door with keys in the lock, a coat on a hook, a pair of headphones hanging on the handle

**Say:**

Let us go through the boxes.

## Slide 7 | radial

**On screen:** Decide. Influence. Respond.

**Say:**

Some things you decide outright. Where your phone is. When you start. Where you sit. What the first task is. Nobody else gets a vote on these.

Some things you can only influence. You can ask the people you live with for quiet. You can agree a signal, like headphones on means not now. You can ask your manager to move a meeting. You cannot guarantee any of it, because other people have their own lives.

And some things you can only respond to. A late train. A sick child. A boiler that breaks. An urgent call from work. You do not get to stop these. You get to choose how you respond.

The limit on this tool: influence is partial. Do not pretend you control everything, and do not collapse into thinking you control nothing. Both mistakes are comfortable, and both cost you.

## Slide 8 | scene

**On screen:** FOUR CONDITIONS YOU DECIDE.
**Visual:** scene: a clean desk under a lamp, a single index card propped against the monitor reading "Write test cases for login page", the phone nowhere in sight

**Say:**

There are four conditions almost everyone can decide.

## Slide 9 | list

**On screen:** PLACE. TIME. FIRST TASK. DISTRACTION.

**Say:**

Place. The same spot, set up before you need it. A corner of the kitchen table counts, as long as it is ready when the session begins.

Time. A fixed start written in the calendar. Whenever you feel ready is not a time.

First task. Written down the night before, and small enough to begin straight away. Work on the project is too vague. Draft the first three questions for the survey is a first task.

Distraction. Phone in another room or in a drawer, notifications off, the browser tabs from earlier closed.

None of these needs willpower in the moment. You set them once, before the session, when you are calm. Then the session runs on the set-up.

## Slide 10 | scene

**On screen:** CAN'T GUARANTEE A QUIET EVENING. CAN GUARANTEE A READY DESK.
**Visual:** scene (Jun in a shared flat, a flatmate's friends laughing in the kitchen behind a half-open door, Jun at his desk with headphones on)

**Say:**

Here is Jun. He is a junior software tester and he is teaching himself automation, because it is the skill that will move him up. His Doom Project is a small portfolio of automated tests.

Jun shares a flat with two others. He cannot guarantee an uninterrupted evening. His flatmates have their own lives, their own friends and their own idea of a good Tuesday.

## Slide 11 | compare

**On screen:** Three plans for the same evening

**Say:**

He has three possible plans.

Hope. He sits down and relies on willpower. The first knock at the door, the first burst of laughter from the kitchen, ends the session. He tells himself he will do double tomorrow.

Demand total control. He gets angry at every noise. He sends tense messages to the flat group chat. He spends the session managing other people, and the atmosphere in the flat gets worse every week.

The if-then plan. He sets the four conditions he decides: phone in the drawer, notifications off, first task on a card, desk clear. He asks his flatmates, once and politely, whether Tuesday and Thursday evenings could be a bit quieter. And he predicts the most likely interruption and decides his response in advance. If the flat gets loud, then I put on headphones and run the twenty-five minute version of the session.

The third plan accepts what he cannot control and prepares for it. That is the fortress.

## Slide 12 | scene

**On screen:** IF THIS HAPPENS, THEN I DO THAT.
**Visual:** scene: an index card on a desk with handwriting: "IF the flat gets loud, THEN headphones + 25-minute version"

**Say:**

The if-then plan is the most useful thing in this lesson, so let us slow down on it.

There is reasonable research suggesting that plans written in this form, if this happens then I will do that, can help people turn intentions into action. They are not magic and they do not guarantee anything. But they move the decision out of the difficult moment and into a calm one.

When the interruption arrives, you do not have to think. You have already thought.

## Slide 13 | story

**On screen:** The schedule changes every week. The rule does not.

**Say:**

A second case. Marcus drives a bus. His shifts rotate: early one week, late the next, split shifts thrown in. No two weeks look the same, so every fixed-evening plan he has ever made has broken by Wednesday.

He used to take that as proof that people like him could not do this kind of thing.

He cannot control the rota. That sits in the respond box. So he moved his effort to the decide box. He keeps a packed bag with his notebook and a tablet. And he has one rule: within two hours of waking, forty minutes on the project, whatever the shift.

On early weeks that is half past one in the afternoon. On late weeks it is ten in the morning. The schedule changes every week. The rule does not. Six weeks later he has a finished plan for the minibus business he has talked about for ten years.

## Slide 15 | red

**On screen:** DESPISE COMFORT. SEEK THE FRICTION.

**Say:**

Principle thirteen says: despise comfort and seek the friction.

In this lesson it has a very practical meaning. Put friction in front of distraction, and take friction away from the work.

A phone in another room takes effort to check. That effort is friction, and it is working for you. A card with your first task already written removes the effort of starting. That is friction taken away from the thing you want.

Comfort here means the easy default: the phone in your hand, the vague task, the sofa. Despising it means refusing to let the easy default make your decisions. It does not mean despising rest, and it never means making yourself suffer for the sake of it.

## Slide 17 | list

**On screen:** WASTED EFFORT.

**Say:**

Fighting the weather. They pour energy into things they can only respond to. Complaining about the rota, the flatmates, the commute. It feels like doing something and it changes nothing.

The perfect set-up. They buy a new desk, a new app and a new notebook, spend an evening arranging them, and still do not start. The set-up became the work.

No plan B. One interruption and the whole evening is written off. A shorter session still counts. Twenty-five minutes on a bad night beats zero minutes waiting for a good one.

And controlling people. They take control and influence and use it as permission to boss everyone around them. Influence means asking. The people you live with get a request and some respect, and the final say over their own evenings.

## Slide 18 | scene

**On screen:** SET IT ONCE. WALK IN. WORK.
**Visual:** scene: a man closing a drawer with his phone inside it, his other hand already on the laptop

**Say:**

Three things to keep.

Decide your conditions once, before the session, when you are calm. Influence what you can by asking, and respond calmly to the rest. And always carry an if-then plan for the interruption you can already see coming.

## Slide 19 | steps

**On screen:** DRAW YOUR CONTROL MAP.

**Say:**

Pause the video when I tell you to.

One. List everything that has broken a session in the last two weeks. Be honest. The phone counts. So does your own tiredness.

Two. Sort each one into decide, influence or respond.

Three. Choose one condition you decide and set it starting tonight. Phone location is a good first choice for most people.

Four. Write one if-then plan for the interruption that is most likely. If this happens, then I will do that. Make the "then" something small and specific you can actually do.

Pause now and give it five minutes.
