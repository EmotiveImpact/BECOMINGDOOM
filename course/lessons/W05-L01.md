## Slide 2 | scene

**On screen:** SAME HOURS. SAME MONEY. SAME YOU.

You only have so many hours, so much money and so much attention. That will not change much in the next two weeks. It probably will not change much in the next two years.

## Slide 3 | question

**On screen:** Where could one hour do the work of ten?

**Say:**

So the question is where one of those scarce resources could produce far more useful output than it does now.

Where could one hour of your effort do the work of ten? Where could one pound do the work of five?

## Slide 4 | scene

**On screen:** A LEVER MOVES MORE THAN YOUR ARM CAN.
**Visual:** scene: a man using a long crowbar to lift a heavy paving slab in a garden, the slab tilting up easily

**Say:**

A lever is the oldest machine there is. With a long enough bar and the right point to push against, one person can move a weight they could never lift with their arms.

The same is true of work. Some effort moves things a little. Some effort, placed in the right spot, moves things a great deal and keeps moving them after you have stopped pushing.

## Slide 5 | red

**On screen:** WHERE CAN A SCARCE RESOURCE PRODUCE MORE USEFUL OUTPUT?

**Say:**

The seventh thinking tool is leverage. The question: where can a scarce resource produce more useful output?

Look hard at the word "useful". Leverage is measured by the result your audience gets. How busy you look, how impressive your tools are and how many hours you put in do not count. Only the result the person at the other end receives.

## Slide 6 | scene

**On screen:** CLARITY. SYSTEMS. TOOLS. PEOPLE.
**Visual:** scene: a single printed checklist pinned to a noticeboard in a staff kitchen, a new starter reading it with a mug in hand

**Say:**

Leverage tends to come in four forms.

## Slide 7 | list

**On screen:** FOUR KINDS OF LEVER.

**Say:**

Clarity. Write something once, clearly, so you never have to explain it again. A good guide, a clear price list, a well-written page of instructions.

Systems. A template, a checklist or a process that other people can follow without you.

Tools. Software that repeats a clear task for you. Your Doombot from week three lives here.

People. Help, paid or swapped, on the parts that others do better or more cheaply than you.

Notice the order. Clarity usually comes first, because every other lever multiplies whatever it is given. A system built on confusion spreads confusion. A tool fed unclear instructions repeats unclear instructions to more people, faster.

## Slide 8 | scene

**On screen:** KNOW THE PROBLEM BEFORE YOU MULTIPLY.
**Visual:** scene: two people at a table, one talking and gesturing while the other writes down exactly what they say in a notebook

**Say:**

Before you multiply anything, be sure you know the problem your audience actually has. The one they describe when you ask them, in their own words, which is often quite different from the one you assumed.

This is week three's first principles, coming back. What is required, observed or assumed? Leverage applied to an assumed problem just produces the wrong thing faster.

## Slide 9 | story

**On screen:** Fifteen questions. Every new starter. Five hours each.

**Say:**

Here is Priya. She is operations manager at a charity that has grown from twelve staff to thirty in a year. Every new starter asks her the same fifteen questions in their first week. Where are the policies? How do I book leave? Who approves expenses? How does the volunteer rota work?

Each new starter costs her about five hours of answering, spread across the week in small interruptions. They are hiring two people a month. That is ten hours a month of the same answers.

Her Doom Project is to fix this. Her first idea is an onboarding chatbot that answers the questions automatically.

## Slide 10 | scene

**On screen:** AUTOMATE IT. ANSWER IT. OR CLARIFY IT.
**Visual:** scene (Priya at her desk, a messy shared drive open on screen with folders named "NEW", "new new", "OLD DO NOT USE")

**Say:**

She has three ways to spend her leverage.

## Slide 11 | compare

**On screen:** Three ways to spend the lever

**Say:**

Automate straight away. She builds the chatbot. But the current instructions are scattered across four folders and two of them contradict each other. So the bot gives confident, confusing answers to more people, faster. Now new starters are confused and they think the answers are official.

Keep answering by hand. Five hours per starter, for ever. It is reliable and it never scales. Every time the charity grows, Priya loses more of her week.

Clarify first. She sits with the last two new starters and writes down their actual questions in their actual words. She writes one clear onboarding checklist, two pages long, with links to the right documents. She tests it with the next starter and asks them to note every question the checklist did not answer.

The questions drop from fifteen to three. She fixes those three. Only then, if it still makes sense, will she automate the parts that are stable.

## Slide 12 | scene

**On screen:** AUTOMATING BAD WORK SCALES BAD WORK.
**Visual:** scene: a factory conveyor belt carrying identical boxes, every box dented in the same place

**Say:**

That first option is the limit on this tool. Automating bad work scales bad work.

And count the hidden costs before you build any lever. Quality: will it be as good as doing it by hand? Maintenance: who updates it when things change? A chatbot nobody maintains becomes wrong within months. A checklist with no owner gets out of date. Every lever needs looking after.

## Slide 13 | red

**On screen:** MANIPULATE CHAOS INTO CURRENCY.

**Say:**

Principle ten says: manipulate chaos into currency.

The chaos in Priya's office was fifteen repeated questions, constant interruptions and contradictory folders. Most people would just put up with it, or complain about it. She turned it into something of value: a checklist that saves hours for every person who joins after.

That is what this principle means in practice. Find a useful response inside the mess. It never means exploiting someone else's distress, and it does not assume every problem can be turned into money. Sometimes the currency is time, trust or calm.

## Slide 14 | scene

**On screen:** THE SAME TEXT AT ELEVEN AT NIGHT.
**Visual:** scene: a young man in a gym hoodie on his sofa late at night, phone lighting his face, a stream of client messages on screen

**Say:**

A second case.

Jamal is a personal trainer with twenty-two clients. Every one of them texts him food questions. Can I eat this? Is this enough protein? What should I have before a session? Often late at night. He answers every one, because he cares, and because he worries they will leave if he does not.

## Slide 15 | story

**On screen:** Made once. Used every day.

**Say:**

He looks for the lever. He collects a month of the questions and finds that about eighty per cent are versions of the same eight.

So he makes a two-page food guide that answers those eight clearly, and three short videos filmed on his phone in the gym. He sends them to every client and pins them in their chat.

Within a month the late-night texts have roughly halved. He knows because he counted them before and after, instead of guessing. His clients get better answers, faster, at any hour. He gets his evenings back. And now that he can see what is working, he can decide what to build next.

This is where principle five, build your empire, starts to mean something concrete.

## Slide 17 | red

**On screen:** AN EMPIRE IS BUILT FROM USEFUL THINGS.

**Say:**

Build your empire. In this course an empire is built from useful assets, systems and fair exchanges. Jamal's guide is an asset. Priya's checklist is an asset. Each one keeps producing value after the effort that made it is over.

It does not mean your worth is measured in money, and nobody here is promised a fortune. Some of you will build a business. Some will build a better role at work, a community project, a body of work. Every one of those is an empire if it is built from useful things.

## Slide 18 | scene

**On screen:** TODAY. CHANGE. EXPECT. BY WHEN.
**Visual:** scene: a hypothesis written on an index card in four short lines, the last one ending in a red question mark

**Say:**

Before you build your lever, write it as a hypothesis you can test. Four short lines.

Today it costs this much. I will change this one thing. I expect this result. I will know by this date.

Leave the result as a question mark until reality fills it in. You are making a prediction, and next lesson you will build the loop that checks it.

## Slide 19 | list

**On screen:** FAKE LEVERAGE.

**Say:**

Here is where people go wrong.

Busy tools. Software that looks impressive and saves nobody any time. If you spend longer managing the tool than it saves, it is a hobby.

Scaling the mess. Multiplying a process before it works. Fix it, then scale it.

Forgotten upkeep. Ignoring the hours it takes to maintain the lever. Count them before you build.

And the wrong audience. A lever that saves you time but makes your audience's experience worse. If Jamal had replaced all his replies with a link and stopped talking to clients, he would have saved time and lost them. The lever has to improve their result, not only yours.

## Slide 20 | scene

**On screen:** CLARITY IS USUALLY THE FIRST LEVER.
**Visual:** scene: a two-page printed guide on a gym bench beside a water bottle, a client reading it between sets

**Say:**

Three things to keep.

Leverage is measured by the result your audience gets. Fix a process before you scale it. And clarity is usually the first lever, because it makes every other one work.

## Slide 21 | steps

**On screen:** FIND YOUR LEVER.

**Say:**

Pause the video when I tell you to.

One. Name your audience in one specific sentence. Who exactly is your Doom Project for?

Two. Write their problem in their words. Use what they have actually said to you, from your conversations in weeks three and four. If you do not have their words yet, that is your first job.

Three. Find the step where the most effort is wasted, either for you or for them.

Four. Write one leverage hypothesis in four lines. Today it costs. I will change. I expect. I will know by.

Pause now and give it five minutes.
